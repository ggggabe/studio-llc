How to studio

Everything about running the studio as a company, in three parts: what we agree to as owners, how the room runs day to day, and how we get it open. Read and understand as much as you can of The Agreement Manual and the Studio Operating Manual before the meeting.

Objectives

  • Keys to the best recording studio in Jersey City.
  • Protection from conflict.
  • Longevity.
  • Big leagues potential.
  • Our fair share when things pop off.

A handshake between homies can't guarantee this.
An LLC with an Operating Agreement and a Studio Operating Manual can.

Glossary

Every term this document leans on, alphabetical. Each links to where it's actually used.

83(b) election
A form filed with the IRS within 30 days of receiving ownership for work, so it's taxed now at its low value instead of later. No extensions. See more
Active partner
A partner who does real work for the business, not just records in it. Active partners can deduct their share of losses against other income federally. See more
Assignment / sublease / new lease
The three ways a lease can come to us from the current tenant. New lease is cleanest; assignment means inheriting their lease as-is; sublease means they stay on the hook and we're only as secure as they are. See more
Basis
Each partner's tax record of what they've put in. Contributions raise it; it's not income and not a deduction. See more
Books
The studio's bookkeeping: every dollar in and out of the bank account, fees, rent, bills, distributions. Kept for the accountant and for taxes. Not the ledger, which tracks contributions and ownership. The two meet only at cash contributions, which appear in both. See more
Breakeven
The point where the studio's outside income covers rent and bills, keeps the reserve full, and can pay fair rates for all the work it still needs. Not the same as being slightly profitable. See more
Business
Outside money you bring to the studio: clients (15% of what they pay), sponsorships, grants, and deals (10% of what lands), or loans and investment (5% of what lands). Counted when the money arrives, as slices or as a cash finder's fee. Exchange rate 2x. See more
Buyout
How a departing partner's ownership is bought back after the freeze: 1.5x annual profit times their share, paid over 24 months, capped so it can't starve the rent. See more
Calendar is king
The studio-time rule: first to book has the room, partner or client, and nobody bumps anybody. See more
Capital call
A request for partners to put in money when the studio genuinely runs short. Split by ownership share; the cash counts as a contribution. Unpaid after 90 days triggers dilution. See more
Cash
Money put into the LLC, or an expense you paid personally and didn't get reimbursed for. Counted at face value, exchange rate 4x. The monthly fee is not cash in this sense; it's a payment for the room. See more
Cause
The short, specific list of things that justify removing a partner: theft or fraud, a capital call unpaid 90+ days after notice, conduct that gets the studio evicted or uninsurable. Using the room a lot is not cause. See more
Certificate of insurance
Proof of the studio's insurance that venues and other businesses ask for before letting us load in or work with them. See more
Claim on future profit
What a partner who leaves before breakeven keeps: their earned slices as a non-voting stake that pays out only if the studio becomes profitable. Not a cash payout at the door. See more
Client
Books the room by the hour or per session. See more
Commission
15% of what a client pays the studio, earned by whoever brought the client in. A partner takes it as slices or as cash; a regular takes cash. See more
Contribution
Anything given to the company beyond the monthly fee: cash, unreimbursed expenses, work, gear, clients, the lease guarantee. Every contribution earns slices. See more
Contribution type
Which of the five kinds a contribution is: cash, work, gear, business, or risk. The type decides how the contribution gets its dollar value and which exchange rate applies. See more
Dilution
When new units are issued and everyone else's percentage goes down even though their unit count didn't. Fair when the new person brought real value; unfair when they didn't. See more
EIN
Employer Identification Number: the LLC's federal tax ID. Free from the IRS, needed to open the bank account. See more
Exchange rate
What the multipliers amount to: one dollar of cash equals two dollars of work. Set by vote before anyone contributes; a later change applies going forward only. See more
Fee
A payment for use of the room. The monthly fee partners pay, or what a client pays per hour. Fees are income to the studio and deductible for the payer. They never earn slices. See more
Freeze
What happens once the studio can pay its own way: the ownership calculation stops, everyone's percentage becomes fixed, and units are issued to match. After the freeze, work is paid in money. See more
Gear
Equipment you own, either sold to the LLC for ownership (resale value, once) or lent to it for ownership (3% of resale value every month it's in the room). Lending for free is also allowed and earns nothing. Exchange rate 2x. See more
Good leaver / bad leaver
A distinction this agreement mostly doesn't make. Everyone who leaves keeps every slice they earned. The only difference is removal for cause, where actual damages are deducted from the claim or buyout. See more
K-1
The yearly tax form the LLC sends each partner showing their share of profit or loss. See more
Lease guarantee
A personal promise to the landlord to cover the rent if the LLC can't. Whoever signs it is taking real risk, so it counts as a contribution, valued at one to three months' rent. See more
Ledger
The shared record of every contribution: date, who, what, value, slices. The whole system depends on it being kept. See more
Limited liability
The legal protection an LLC gives its owners: the company's debts and lawsuits can't reach personal savings, as long as the company is run as a real company and nobody personally guaranteed the debt. See more
LLC
Limited liability company. The legal entity that is the studio: it signs the lease, owns the gear, holds the bank account, and keeps its problems away from anyone's personal savings. See more
Majority of units and two people
The voting rule for big decisions: more than half the ownership and at least two partners must agree. Stops one big owner from acting alone and one small owner from blocking alone. See more
Manager
The partner who runs day to day: bills, approvals, the landlord, the monthly close. A job, not an ownership level. Can be replaced by vote. See more
Manager-managed
An LLC where one named person runs operations and the owners vote only on listed big decisions. The alternative, member-managed, means everyone votes on everything. See more
Mediation / arbitration
The two steps before court for a deadlock: a neutral third party helps you agree (non-binding), then a private arbitrator decides (binding). Faster and cheaper than a lawsuit. See more
Member
New Jersey's legal word for an owner of an LLC. In this document it only ever means that. A partner is a member; a regular is not. See more
Membership interest
The legal term for owning part of an LLC, in New Jersey law and in the operating agreement. What this document calls ownership or equity, and what becomes units at the freeze. Expect the lawyer's draft to say this. See more
Monthly close
The manager's monthly routine, about an hour: reconcile the bank, tag every deposit, approve hours, update the pie, post the split. See more
Monthly fee
The flat amount every partner pays each month for unlimited use of the room. Set in a band between what everyone can reliably pay and what the access is worth, same for everyone, changed only by vote. It is not a contribution: it buys use of the room, earns no slices, and is deductible for the payer. Anything paid above it counts as a contribution unless you say otherwise. See more
Multiplier
The weight applied to a contribution's dollar value to turn it into slices: 4x for cash, 2x for work, gear, business, and the lease guarantee. It sets the exchange rate between cash and everything else. See more
Open for business
The point where the studio is actually available to customers: a fee sheet, a way to book, a usable room, someone paying. Turns spending from startup costs into normal expenses. See more
Operating agreement
The contract between the owners that sets the rules: ownership, money, votes, leaving. A lawyer writes it from this document. Without one, the state's defaults apply. See more
Ownership
Your percentage of the company. Pre-freeze it's your slices out of all slices that exist, and it moves monthly. Post-freeze it's your units out of all units, and it's fixed. Same thing as equity; the legal term is membership interest. See more
Partner
Owns a piece of the studio. Pays the flat monthly fee, earns ownership for what they put in beyond it, votes on big decisions. Legally, an LLC member. Out in the world: a co-owner. See more
Pass-through
How an LLC is taxed by default: the company pays no income tax itself; profit or loss passes to each owner's personal return. See more
Permitted use
The lease clause saying what the space may be used for. Ours must say recording studio, with 24-hour access and public sessions, in writing. See more
Per-owner fee
New Jersey's $150 per owner per year on partnerships with more than two owners. The studio pays it, not the partners. See more
Post-freeze
The second phase. The studio's income covers rent, bills, the reserve, and fair pay for its work, so the calculation stops: percentages become units and stay fixed, work is paid in money, and ownership changes only by vote. See more
Pre-emptive rights
When new units are issued, existing partners get first crack at buying their share, so anyone who wants to hold their percentage can. See more
Pre-freeze
The first phase. The studio can't yet pay for the work it needs, so it pays in ownership: contributions become slices, and percentages move every month. All of the Ownership model section is pre-freeze rules. See more
Profits interest
A form of ownership granted for work that's taxed on future growth rather than on the day it's received. The plan avoids needing it by not issuing units until the freeze. See more
Project pricing
Valuing a piece of work with a finish line as a fixed amount agreed up front, like a contractor's quote, instead of logging hours. Slices on completion. See more
Regular
Uses the room on a flat monthly fee but owns none of it. No slices, no vote. Can bring in clients for a cash commission. See more
Reserve
Cash the studio keeps on hand, target two to three months of rent, before anything is distributed. What makes a bad month survivable. See more
Reserved pool
Units set aside at the freeze for future partners, so bringing someone in doesn't dilute anyone until the pool is used up. See more
Risk
Signing the lease guarantee: personally promising the landlord the rent if the LLC can't pay. Valued at one to three months of rent, agreed up front. Exchange rate 2x. See more
Section 179
The federal rule letting a business write off gear in full the year it's bought. New Jersey caps it at $25,000 a year. See more
Self-employment tax
Roughly 15% owed by active partners on their share of profit, on top of income tax. See more
Shotgun clause
A deadlock breaker between two partners: one names a price per unit, the other must buy or sell at it. Forces an honest number. Optional. See more
Slice
Earned through contributions in the form of cash, work, gear, business, and risk. A slice represents what you've put into the studio, measured in one unit so those five things can be compared. Slices aren't money and can't be cashed out; they only mean something relative to everyone else's. Your ownership percentage is your slices out of all slices that exist, and it moves every month. Slices only go up while you're a partner. See more
Slicing Pie
A published model for splitting a company by what people actually put in, instead of negotiating percentages up front. Everything in the Ownership model section runs on it. See more
Startup costs
Everything spent before the business opens. Only $5,000 is deductible in the first year; the rest trickles out over 15 years. Everything after opening day is a normal expense. See more
Sunk costs
Money or work spent before the LLC existed on things the studio never receives: an old space, lessons, your own records, gear that didn't come with you. Not a contribution, however much it was. Gear that's physically in the room on the start date is the one exception; it goes through the normal gear options at today's value. See more
Supermajority
More than a simple majority, usually two thirds. Required here to issue units beyond the reserved pool. See more
Sweat equity
Ownership earned by working for the studio: wiring, buildout, engineering for clients, booking, cleaning. Logged weekly, approved monthly, valued at the rate for that kind of work, counted at 2x. See more
Tripwire
Any partner's right to call a review of studio-time usage, about the system or about one person's use of the room. See more
Units
The fixed pieces of ownership issued at the freeze. Your percentage is your units divided by all units issued. Easier to add a partner with than percentages. See more
W-9
The tax form a business hands over to get paid by another business. Having one in the LLC's name is part of being fundable. See more
Work
Hours for the studio logged at the rate for that kind of work, or a project priced up front. Not work on your own music. Exchange rate 2x. See sweat equity. See more
Worker
Someone paid to do a job for the studio without owning any of it: a contractor at first, an employee only once there's steady revenue. Not a partner, so no slices and no ledger line. See more
