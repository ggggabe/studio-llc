Studio operating manual

How we run the place day to day: the room, the gear, moving money, and the monthly close.

1Booking the studio

Fully open, calendar is king.

First to book has the room

What
One shared calendar. Whoever books a slot first has it, whether that's a partner or a paying client. No hour limits for partners, and no tracking: nobody logs or counts how much anyone uses the room. (Work hours for the studio are a different thing and are logged, because they earn ownership. Use hours never are.)
Why
Partners want the room to feel like theirs, not like a gym with a punch card. Clients need to know a booking is a booking. First-come handles both without anyone counting hours.
How
Put your time on the calendar before you show up. If it's blank, it's available to anyone, including a client the manager books. If it's yours, it's yours.
Rules
Nobody bumps anybody, in either direction. A partner can't take a client's slot, and a client can't take a partner's.

The tripwire

What
Any partner can call a usage review at any time, about the system or about one person. If someone's use of the room looks egregious (the room is theirs five nights a week, clients can't get in, gear's always set up for their session), any partner can ask for it to be looked at. Calling the review is a right; deciding anything is a regular vote.
Why
Fully open works until it doesn't. The tripwire means nobody has to feel like the bad guy for raising it, and nobody gets ambushed by a rule change they didn't see coming.
How
  • Reviewing one person's use: say so at the monthly close. The calendar is the record, so it's a look at the last two or three months, not an argument about memory. Outcome is a conversation first: an agreement on how that person books going forward, or nothing if it turns out fine.
  • If it doesn't resolve: the regular vote (majority of units and at least two people) can set a limit for that partner (nights per week, or no more than X hours in the next 30 days) for a fixed period, or switch the whole room to included blocks. No special threshold; same rule as every other decision.
  • Reviewing the system: same place, same vote. A switch from fully open to included blocks applies to everyone.
Rules
Using the room a lot is not "cause" for anything (see Removal). It's a scheduling problem, handled here. Also revisit if a new partner joins at a very different ownership level than the others, since fully open assumes everyone's roughly in the same boat.

2Gear

What's in the room, who owns it, how it goes out to clients, and how it gets bought and fixed. The value side of gear a partner brings in is in Contributions.

The gear list

Everything in and everything out, one line each. The lists are whatever's in without an out.

What
One shared sheet, kept by the manager, that works like the ledger does for contributions: a log, not a snapshot. Every item gets a line the day it comes in: date, item, serial, owner (the LLC or a partner), agreed value, condition, and how it came in: bought by the studio, or a partner's own gear sold to the studio, lent free, or lent for ownership. It gets another line the day it goes out: date, where, who has it, and why: rented to a client, out for repair, back to its owner, sold. What's in the room right now is every item with an in and no out.
How
Two views of the same sheet: what the LLC owns, and the loan list, what's in the room but still a partner's. Nothing comes in without the manager's yes and a line (see Gear contributions for how value and ownership are agreed). Nothing leaves, even for a night, without a line. The insurance schedule is built from what the LLC owns.
Rules
If it isn't on the list, it isn't in the studio: no "I bought it on my card and it lives here." Partners use everything on both lists for their own sessions; personal gear lent free is used with the owner's standing okay, noted on its line. An item that comes back from a rental or a repair gets a condition note on the way in.

Renting gear to clients

What
A client taking the studio's gear out of the building for a job elsewhere. Gear in the room comes with the booking and is never rented separately. Prices and invoicing are in Moving money; this is how it's handled.
Why
Gear sitting idle earns nothing, and gear walking out the door without paperwork is how it disappears.
How

Sign-out: the manager signs off. A dated sign-out sheet lists each item and serial, the client signs it, and a deposit is taken before anything moves. Condition is checked and photographed on the way out and the way back.

Damage or loss: the deposit first, the studio's insurance second. The client is invoiced for the difference.

Rules
LLC-owned gear goes out by default. Partner gear on the loan list is rented to clients only with the owner's okay, recorded once on the list; lent-free gear never leaves the room with a client. Rental income is the studio's either way; a partner lending for ownership keeps their monthly credit and nothing more.

Borrowing gear as a partner

In the room it's yours to use. Out of the room it's signed out, free.

What
Everything on the gear list is there for partners to use in their own sessions; the fee covers it. Taking the studio's gear out for a show or a session elsewhere is covered too, at no charge, as long as the room doesn't need it.
How
Same sign-out as a client rental, minus the money: a line on the gear list with what, who, and when it's back, and a message to the manager. No deposit, no invoice. Check the calendar first: if a session is booked that needs it, it stays.
Rules

Booked sessions win. Gear is back before the next booking that needs it, not the next time you're around.

Normal-use damage is the studio's (see Liability). Loss or carelessness, like a mic left in a van overnight, is the partner's, up to the insurance deductible.

Your own lent gear is yours to take, any time, no charge. Log it out so the room knows, and if it's lent for ownership it earns nothing for the months it's gone.

Not for lending onward. A partner doesn't take studio gear out for a friend; the friend is a client and rents it themselves.

Buying new gear

What
When the studio needs something new, the LLC buys it from the LLC's account, in the LLC's name.
Why
So the receipt, the insurance, and the write-off are all the studio's, and the item clearly stays if a partner leaves.
How
Any partner proposes it. Inside the buildout budget, or under $2,000 with a heads-up to the partners, the manager buys it; $2,000 and over is a regular vote. If a partner is funding it, they put the cash into the LLC first as a cash contribution and the LLC pays the vendor. Receipt in the shared folder, item on the gear list the day it arrives.
Rules
No partner buys studio gear on a personal card and hands it over; that's either a reimbursement or a contribution, and it's decided before the purchase. Where possible keep single items under $2,500 so they can be written off at once (see Timing).

Maintenance

Keeping the room working. The studio pays, not whoever noticed.

Consumables are a studio cost. Strings, drumheads, cables, tape, batteries, cleaning supplies, coffee. Kept stocked from the account, with a running list of what's low that anyone can add to.

Repairs are a studio cost. A blown speaker, a dead channel, a tech visit, a plumber. Under $200 the manager just handles it. Over that, a quick message to the partners first, and $2,000 or over it's a vote.

Say something the day it breaks. A broken item gets a note on its gear list line and a tag on the item, so the next session doesn't find out the hard way. Nobody's in trouble for breaking something in normal use (see Liability); people are in trouble for hiding it.

If a partner fronts something urgent, they drop the receipt in the shared folder and pick one: get reimbursed (no ownership earned) or log it as a contribution (earns ownership, no reimbursement). Either is fine; it has to be written down. No receipt, no reimbursement and no contribution.

Routine upkeep is logged work. Cleaning, cable checks, swapping tubes, re-stringing the house guitars, backing up the session drive. A partner who does it logs the hours at the admin or maintenance rate like any other work.

Leave it how you found it. Patch bay normalled, mics in their cases, console zeroed, trash out. The room is everyone's next session.

Liability

What's insured, whose gear, and who pays when something breaks.

General liability covers people getting hurt and damage to other people's property. It's what the landlord and venues want the certificate of insurance for.

Property coverage, usually an inland marine policy for gear, covers the studio's equipment against theft, fire, water, and damage, in the room and on the road.

The LLC's gear is scheduled on the property policy from the gear list. Anything over an agreed value is scheduled individually when it comes in.

Partner gear on the loan list is covered while it's in the room, as property of others in the studio's care. That's the promise behind "the LLC insures it while it's there" in Gear contributions, and it only holds if the list is current.

Client gear in the room is the client's responsibility unless the studio is handling it. The booking says so.

Wear and tear is a studio repair, paid from the account (see Maintenance).

A client damaged it: their deposit, then insurance, then an invoice for the difference.

A partner damaged lent gear in normal use: the studio repairs or replaces it. Nobody's personal money.

Theft, fire, flood: a claim, with the gear list as the schedule. The deductible is a studio expense.

Gear leaving the building is only covered if the policy says off-premises, so that's checked before the first rental. And no gear in the room before a policy is bound, the stopgap company's or the studio's.

3Moving money

What the studio gets paid, by whom, and how money goes out again. All of it goes through the LLC's account and is the studio's money, kept in the books; it is never anyone's contribution. It pays the bills, builds the reserve, and what's left is distributed by ownership share (see Where does the money go?).

Where things get recorded

Four records. Every card below says which one each thing goes in.

The books. Every dollar in and out of the LLC's account, tagged: fee, client, rental, event, sponsorship, grant, loan, reimbursement, expense, distribution. The bookkeeping tool plus the bank statement. The accountant works from this (see Books).

The gear list. Every item in and out of the room, including rentals and sign-outs (see The gear list).

The calendar. Every booking and event, with the deposit status on the entry (see Booking the studio).

The shared folder. The paper: invoices sent, receipts, signed agreements, sign-out sheets, count sheets, W-9s. One subfolder per month.

Partner fees

The price of access. Same for everyone, every month.

What it is
The flat monthly amount every partner pays the studio for access to the room and borrowing gear.
Price
The same for every partner, set in the band (see How is the monthly fee set?).
How
Due on the 1st. Set up recurring Zelle or bill pay from your bank to the LLC's account, memo "studio access" and the month (see How does money actually move?). The LLC sends the invoice the same day; keep it for your taxes.
The manager records it: invoice sent on the 1st and saved to the shared folder; the deposit matched to it in the books and tagged "fee" at the monthly close; any shortfall noted in the books against that partner.
Rules

It covers your own sessions and your use of the studio's gear, in the room and out of it. It never covers a client's session you're engineering, and it never buys ownership.

Never a contribution. Anything paid above it is one, by default (see The fee).

Miss a month and nothing bad happens. Tell the manager before the 1st, the shortfall is noted, the reserve covers it, and you earn no extra ownership that month (see What if I can't pay one month?).

At 90 days unpaid, the other partners cover the gap as a capital call and the unpaid partner's share is diluted by the ordinary pie math. That's the only penalty, and it's three months out.

No late fees between partners. The studio isn't a landlord.

No sales tax on room access.

Regular fees

Access without ownership.

What it is
The flat monthly amount a regular pays for access to the room. Same calendar, same gear, same open booking as a partner. No slices, no vote, no K-1.
Price
The manager sets it on the fee sheet, usually higher than a partner's; like any fee-sheet number, a regular vote can change it. Not in the partners' band; a regular pays what the access is worth.
How
Same mechanics as a partner: due on the 1st, bank to bank or by the invoice's pay button, invoice from the LLC the same day.
The manager records it: invoice in the shared folder, payment tagged "fee, regular" in the books at the close, a missed month noted there and access paused on the calendar.
Rules

It covers their own sessions and the gear in the room. Taking gear out of the building is a client rental.

Nothing above it earns ownership. A regular can't contribute their way in; becoming a partner is a vote and a signature (see Regular).

A missed month is a missed month. Access pauses until it's paid; there's no reserve covering a regular, and no capital call either.

A client a regular brings in earns the 15% commission as a cash finder's fee, never slices.

Client sessions

Outside clients booking the room. What turns a shared space into a business.

What it is
A client who isn't a partner or regular pays for the room by the hour, by the day, or per project.
Price
The fee sheet: hourly, daily, and project rates plus a deposit policy. The manager writes it, a regular vote changes it, and any partner can hand it to a stranger. Discounts for a specific client are the manager's call up to an agreed percentage, logged with the booking.
How
Booked on the calendar like anything else (see Booking the studio). A deposit holds the date; the balance is due before the session starts, or on the invoice's due date for project work. Invoice from the LLC, with any gear going out of the building on its own line, paid by ACH or card, with the card fee priced into the rate (see How does money actually move?). Cancel inside the window on the fee sheet and the deposit is kept; outside it, the deposit moves to a new date. Refunds come only from the manager, from the account. The client pays the studio, never a person.
The manager records it: the booking on the calendar with "deposit paid" on the entry; the invoice in the shared folder; deposit and balance tagged "client" in the books, with the referring partner's name on the entry so the commission can be worked out (see Business).
Who gets paid

The partner who brought the client. 15% of what the client pays, every month they keep paying, taken as ownership or as cash (see Business).

The partner who works the session. Pre-freeze, the hours are logged as work at the engineering rate and earn slices; no cash. Post-freeze, it's a guaranteed payment at a rate set by vote. Default until the partners vote otherwise.

A non-partner engineer. A contractor on a 1099 (see Workers).

Rules
A partner's own sessions are covered by their fee. A partner's outside client pays the client rate even when the partner is the one engineering, and nobody books the room under their own name to give a client a cheaper one. Nobody takes a cut of a client's payment directly; the studio pays the worker.

Gear rental

Studio gear leaving the building with a client. In the room, it comes with the room.

What it is
A client taking the studio's gear out for a job somewhere else. A client who books the room gets everything in it; nothing in the room is rented separately.
Price
Per item, per day, on the fee sheet, plus a deposit.
How
Its own invoice line, with New Jersey sales tax collected on it and passed to the state (see Sales tax). Sign-out, deposits, and damage are handled as in Gear.
The manager records it: an out line and a back line on the gear list; the signed sign-out sheet in the shared folder; the rental tagged "rental" and the sales tax as collected in the books; the deposit in the books as held, then refunded or applied.
Rules
Partners never pay gear rental, in the room or out of it; it's in the fee (see Borrowing gear as a partner). Rental income on gear a partner lent for ownership is the studio's; the lender's monthly credit doesn't change.

Events

Shows, listening parties, workshops. Door money and tickets.

What it is
Anything the studio runs or hosts where people pay to be there: a show in the room, a release party, a class, a studio night out at a venue.
Price
Set per event by whoever is running it with the manager, before it's announced: ticket price, door split, what the acts get.
How
Door money is counted by two people the same night, on a count sheet, and deposited the next business day. Acts, the venue, and the event's costs are paid first; what's left is studio income.
The manager records it: the event on the calendar; the budget and the signed count sheet in the shared folder; the deposit tagged "event" in the books, each payout tagged against the event.
Rules
A partner performing is paid like any act, from the event's budget, not from the pie. Door cash is never in someone's pocket overnight without the count sheet. Events in the room follow the calendar and the noise rules like any booking.

Sponsorships, grants, and deals

Outside money the studio keeps, and outside money it pays back.

What it is
A brand sponsoring the room, a grant, a venue or label deal, a loan, an investor. All of it signed by the LLC and deposited in its account.
Price
Whatever the agreement says. Only money that actually lands counts as income, and only the part the studio keeps: a loan is cash in, not income, and goes back out with interest.
How
The manager tracks the strings: reporting, deliverables, spending rules. Grant money is spent as the grant says and nothing else.
The manager records it: the signed agreement in the shared folder; the money tagged "sponsorship," "grant," or "deal" in the books, or "loan" as a liability, never income; grant spending tagged against the grant so the report writes itself; the referring partner's name on the entry (see Business).
Who gets paid
The partner who brought it: 10% of sponsorships, grants, and deals; 5% of loans and investment. Ownership or cash, chosen on the ledger line when it's claimed (see Business).
Rules
Taking a loan, giving up ownership, or signing a deal worth $2,000 or more needs a vote. A deal that gives anyone outside the studio a say in the room is read by the lawyer first.

Paying out

Money leaving the account. Only the manager moves it.

What
Rent, insurance, utilities, vendors, contractors, reimbursements, cash commissions, and distributions. Every one from the LLC's account, by the manager, with a receipt or invoice behind it.
How
  • Bills and vendors: paid from the account on their due dates, by ACH or bill pay. Nobody pays the landlord or a vendor personally (see The LLC).
  • Contractors: a W-9 before the first payment, paid per job or per hour by ACH, a 1099 in January if they were paid $600 or more (see Workers).
  • Reimbursements: receipt in the shared folder, marked "reimburse," paid back within 14 days of the monthly close. No receipt, no reimbursement.
  • Cash commissions: paid when the client's or sponsor's money lands, as a studio expense (see Business).
  • Distributions: quarterly, by ownership share, once the reserve is at target, enough to cover everyone's tax first (see Where does the money go?).

The manager records it: every payment matched in the books to a receipt or invoice in the shared folder, tagged by what it was; contractor W-9s in the folder; reimbursements and cash commissions are expenses in the books.

Rules
One person moves money out: the manager. A second partner has read access to the account and sees every transaction. Any one-off payment over $200 that isn't rent or a recurring bill gets a message to the partners before it's paid, and anything $2,000 or over gets a vote.

4The monthly close

The manager's routine, once a month on the fixed date. The Agreement's monthly close is the part where contributions become slices; this is everything around it that keeps the studio's money, gear, and calendar straight.

The checklist

In order. About an hour once it's routine.

1. Reconcile the bank. Every transaction on the statement matched to a line in the books with its tag, and to an invoice or receipt in the month's shared folder. Anything unmatched gets chased now, not next month.

2. Fees. Who paid, who didn't. Shortfalls noted against the partner; a regular who hasn't paid has access paused on the calendar (see Partner fees).

3. Clients and rentals. Deposits held, balances due, overdue invoices sent a reminder. Deposits on finished rentals refunded or applied. Sales tax collected this month set aside in its own sub-account for the quarterly return (see Sales tax).

4. Pay out. Reimbursements and cash commissions from the month, contractors, anything else owed (see Paying out).

5. Gear. Walk the gear list: everything signed out is back or has a date; new items are on the insurance schedule; broken items are tagged and in the repair queue; the low-stock list is ordered.

6. Calendar. Next month's bookings and events confirmed, deposits in, and the room isn't double-booked around an event.

7. Contributions. Approve the ledger lines and run the pie, per the Agreement (see Monthly close). Another partner approves the manager's own lines.

8. Reserve and distributions. Reserve balance against target. Quarter end: the tax distribution first, then anything above target by ownership share (see Where does the money go?).

9. Post it, and hear from everyone. The month's summary goes where every partner can see it (below), partners raise anything that looks wrong or needs deciding, and the month's shared folder is closed.

What partners do at the close

The close is the one time a month everyone looks at the same page. Use it.

Before the close: get your lines in. Contributions logged for the month, receipts in the shared folder marked reimburse or contribution, hours entered. Anything missed can be backfilled for three months, but the close is when it's easiest.

One partner approves the manager's lines. The manager can't approve their own contributions or sign off their own cash over the cap; whoever's turn it is does it at the close (see The Ledger).

Read the posted split and say so if it's wrong. A ledger line that looks off, a payment that isn't there, a percentage that doesn't add up: raise it that month. Entries older than three months are presumed settled (see The ledger settles facts).

Dispute an entry. A partner can flag any line at the close; if it's still disagreed after two closes it's marked disputed and stays out of the pie until it's resolved by the manager with evidence, or by a vote.

Call a usage review. If the room is being hogged or clients can't get in, the close is the natural place to pull the tripwire. Calling it is a right; deciding anything is a regular vote.

Bring decisions. Anything on the "decisions pending" list gets talked through, and voted if it needs a vote. A partner who wants something on next month's list tells the manager before the close.

Flag money trouble early. If next month's fee is going to be a problem, say it at the close, not on the 1st (see What if I can't pay one month?).

What gets posted

One page, same layout every month.

Money. Income by source, expenses by kind, the result for the month, the reserve balance against target, and anything owed in or out.

Ownership. The current split after this month's close, and what changed since last month.

Room. Hours booked by clients, events held, anything broken or out on loan.

Decisions pending. Anything that needs a vote or a conversation before next close, so nobody is surprised at the meeting.

Dates

The 1st. Fees due, invoices sent.

The close date. A fixed day between the 5th and the 10th, to be decided at the partner meeting (agenda item 20), same day every month whether or not there's much to close. Late enough that the bank statement and the fees are in, early enough that shortfalls and the posted split are out before the next 1st. If it lands on a weekend, the next business day.

December and January. A dry run on the chosen date: no pie, no fees, just the four records set up, the gear list walked for the insurance quote, and the one page posted with where the lease, the lawyer, and each partner's paperwork stand. If the lease starts early, the money part is real, on the stopgap company's books.

Within 14 days of the close. Reimbursements paid.

Quarterly. New Jersey sales tax return, if the studio collects any. Tax distributions once there's profit.

January. 1099s to contractors paid $600 or more.

March. The partnership return and K-1s, with the accountant (see Documents).

The formation anniversary. New Jersey annual report, paid from the account.

Only when the bank asks. The bank's ownership form names the control person, which is the manager, and anyone who owns 25% or more. It's filled in once at account opening, when the pie is zero and the manager is the only name, and then only when the bank itself asks again: usually a yearly review, or when a signer is added or a loan is taken. Nobody watches the split for this, and crossing 25% at a monthly close triggers nothing. When the bank does ask, the manager reads the split off the last close, names anyone at or above 25%, and those partners send the bank a copy of their ID and their address. It's a bank record, not a tax event, and nothing is filed with the state or the IRS.

Glossary

Every term this document leans on, alphabetical. Each links to where it's actually used.

83(b) election
A form filed with the IRS within 30 days of receiving ownership for work, so it's taxed now at its low value instead of later. No extensions. See more
Active partner
A partner who does real work for the business, not just records in it. Active partners can deduct their share of losses against other income federally. See more
Assignment / sublease / new lease
The three ways a lease can come to us from the current tenant. New lease is cleanest; assignment means inheriting their lease as-is; sublease means they stay on the hook and we're only as secure as they are. See more
Basis
Each partner's tax record of what they've put in. Contributions raise it; it's not income and not a deduction. See more
Books
The studio's bookkeeping: every dollar in and out of the bank account, fees, rent, bills, distributions. Kept for the accountant and for taxes. Not the ledger, which tracks contributions and ownership. The two meet only at cash contributions, which appear in both. See more
Breakeven
The point where the studio's outside income covers rent and bills, keeps the reserve full, and can pay fair rates for all the work it still needs. Not the same as being slightly profitable. See more
Business
Outside money you bring to the studio: clients (15% of what they pay), sponsorships, grants, and deals (10% of what lands), or loans and investment (5% of what lands). Counted when the money arrives, as slices or as a cash finder's fee. Exchange rate 2x. See more
Buyout
How a departing partner's ownership is bought back after the freeze: 1.5x annual profit times their share, paid over 24 months, capped so it can't starve the rent. See more
Calendar is king
The studio-time rule: first to book has the room, partner or client, and nobody bumps anybody. See more
Capital call
A request for partners to put in money when the studio genuinely runs short. Split by ownership share; the cash counts as a contribution. Unpaid after 90 days triggers dilution. See more
Cash
Money put into the LLC, or an expense you paid personally and didn't get reimbursed for. Counted at face value, exchange rate 4x. The monthly fee is not cash in this sense; it's a payment for the room. See more
Cause
The short, specific list of things that justify removing a partner: theft or fraud, a capital call unpaid 90+ days after notice, conduct that gets the studio evicted or uninsurable. Using the room a lot is not cause. See more
Certificate of insurance
Proof of the studio's insurance that venues and other businesses ask for before letting us load in or work with them. See more
Claim on future profit
What a partner who leaves before breakeven keeps: their earned slices as a non-voting stake that pays out only if the studio becomes profitable. Not a cash payout at the door. See more
Client
Books the room by the hour or per session. See more
Commission
15% of what a client pays the studio, earned by whoever brought the client in. A partner takes it as slices or as cash; a regular takes cash. See more
Contribution
Anything given to the company beyond the monthly fee: cash, unreimbursed expenses, work, gear, clients, the lease guarantee. Every contribution earns slices. See more
Contribution type
Which of the five kinds a contribution is: cash, work, gear, business, or risk. The type decides how the contribution gets its dollar value and which exchange rate applies. See more
Dilution
When new units are issued and everyone else's percentage goes down even though their unit count didn't. Fair when the new person brought real value; unfair when they didn't. See more
EIN
Employer Identification Number: the LLC's federal tax ID. Free from the IRS, needed to open the bank account. See more
Exchange rate
What the multipliers amount to: one dollar of cash equals two dollars of work. Set by vote before anyone contributes; a later change applies going forward only. See more
Fee
A payment for use of the room. The monthly fee partners pay, or what a client pays per hour. Fees are income to the studio and deductible for the payer. They never earn slices. See more
Freeze
What happens once the studio can pay its own way: the ownership calculation stops, everyone's percentage becomes fixed, and units are issued to match. After the freeze, work is paid in money. See more
Gear
Equipment you own, either sold to the LLC for ownership (resale value, once) or lent to it for ownership (3% of resale value every month it's in the room). Lending for free is also allowed and earns nothing. Exchange rate 2x. See more
Good leaver / bad leaver
A distinction this agreement mostly doesn't make. Everyone who leaves keeps every slice they earned. The only difference is removal for cause, where actual damages are deducted from the claim or buyout. See more
K-1
The yearly tax form the LLC sends each partner showing their share of profit or loss. See more
Lease guarantee
A personal promise to the landlord to cover the rent if the LLC can't. Whoever signs it is taking real risk, so it counts as a contribution, valued at one to three months' rent. See more
Ledger
The shared record of every contribution: date, who, what, value, slices. The whole system depends on it being kept. See more
Limited liability
The legal protection an LLC gives its owners: the company's debts and lawsuits can't reach personal savings, as long as the company is run as a real company and nobody personally guaranteed the debt. See more
LLC
Limited liability company. The legal entity that is the studio: it signs the lease, owns the gear, holds the bank account, and keeps its problems away from anyone's personal savings. See more
Majority of units and two people
The voting rule for big decisions: more than half the ownership and at least two partners must agree. Stops one big owner from acting alone and one small owner from blocking alone. See more
Manager
The partner who runs day to day: bills, approvals, the landlord, the monthly close. A job, not an ownership level. Can be replaced by vote. See more
Manager-managed
An LLC where one named person runs operations and the owners vote only on listed big decisions. The alternative, member-managed, means everyone votes on everything. See more
Mediation / arbitration
The two steps before court for a deadlock: a neutral third party helps you agree (non-binding), then a private arbitrator decides (binding). Faster and cheaper than a lawsuit. See more
Member
New Jersey's legal word for an owner of an LLC. In this document it only ever means that. A partner is a member; a regular is not. See more
Membership interest
The legal term for owning part of an LLC, in New Jersey law and in the operating agreement. What this document calls ownership or equity, and what becomes units at the freeze. Expect the lawyer's draft to say this. See more
Monthly close
The manager's monthly routine, about an hour: reconcile the bank, tag every deposit, approve hours, update the pie, post the split. See more
Monthly fee
The flat amount every partner pays each month for unlimited use of the room. Set in a band between what everyone can reliably pay and what the access is worth, same for everyone, changed only by vote. It is not a contribution: it buys use of the room, earns no slices, and is deductible for the payer. Anything paid above it counts as a contribution unless you say otherwise. See more
Multiplier
The weight applied to a contribution's dollar value to turn it into slices: 4x for cash, 2x for work, gear, business, and the lease guarantee. It sets the exchange rate between cash and everything else. See more
Open for business
The point where the studio is actually available to customers: a fee sheet, a way to book, a usable room, someone paying. Turns spending from startup costs into normal expenses. See more
Operating agreement
The contract between the owners that sets the rules: ownership, money, votes, leaving. A lawyer writes it from this document. Without one, the state's defaults apply. See more
Ownership
Your percentage of the company. Pre-freeze it's your slices out of all slices that exist, and it moves monthly. Post-freeze it's your units out of all units, and it's fixed. Same thing as equity; the legal term is membership interest. See more
Partner
Owns a piece of the studio. Pays the flat monthly fee, earns ownership for what they put in beyond it, votes on big decisions. Legally, an LLC member. Out in the world: a co-owner. See more
Pass-through
How an LLC is taxed by default: the company pays no income tax itself; profit or loss passes to each owner's personal return. See more
Permitted use
The lease clause saying what the space may be used for. Ours must say recording studio, with 24-hour access and public sessions, in writing. See more
Per-owner fee
New Jersey's $150 per owner per year on partnerships with more than two owners. The studio pays it, not the partners. See more
Post-freeze
The second phase. The studio's income covers rent, bills, the reserve, and fair pay for its work, so the calculation stops: percentages become units and stay fixed, work is paid in money, and ownership changes only by vote. See more
Pre-emptive rights
When new units are issued, existing partners get first crack at buying their share, so anyone who wants to hold their percentage can. See more
Pre-freeze
The first phase. The studio can't yet pay for the work it needs, so it pays in ownership: contributions become slices, and percentages move every month. All of the Ownership model section is pre-freeze rules. See more
Profits interest
A form of ownership granted for work that's taxed on future growth rather than on the day it's received. The plan avoids needing it by not issuing units until the freeze. See more
Project pricing
Valuing a piece of work with a finish line as a fixed amount agreed up front, like a contractor's quote, instead of logging hours. Slices on completion. See more
Regular
Uses the room on a flat monthly fee but owns none of it. No slices, no vote. Can bring in clients for a cash commission. See more
Reserve
Cash the studio keeps on hand, target two to three months of rent, before anything is distributed. What makes a bad month survivable. See more
Reserved pool
Units set aside at the freeze for future partners, so bringing someone in doesn't dilute anyone until the pool is used up. See more
Risk
Signing the lease guarantee: personally promising the landlord the rent if the LLC can't pay. Valued at one to three months of rent, agreed up front. Exchange rate 2x. See more
Section 179
The federal rule letting a business write off gear in full the year it's bought. New Jersey caps it at $25,000 a year. See more
Self-employment tax
Roughly 15% owed by active partners on their share of profit, on top of income tax. See more
Shotgun clause
A deadlock breaker between two partners: one names a price per unit, the other must buy or sell at it. Forces an honest number. Optional. See more
Slice
Earned through contributions in the form of cash, work, gear, business, and risk. A slice represents what you've put into the studio, measured in one unit so those five things can be compared. Slices aren't money and can't be cashed out; they only mean something relative to everyone else's. Your ownership percentage is your slices out of all slices that exist, and it moves every month. Slices only go up while you're a partner. See more
Slicing Pie
A published model for splitting a company by what people actually put in, instead of negotiating percentages up front. Everything in the Ownership model section runs on it. See more
Startup costs
Everything spent before the business opens. Only $5,000 is deductible in the first year; the rest trickles out over 15 years. Everything after opening day is a normal expense. See more
Sunk costs
Money or work spent before the LLC existed on things the studio never receives: an old space, lessons, your own records, gear that didn't come with you. Not a contribution, however much it was. Gear that's physically in the room on the start date is the one exception; it goes through the normal gear options at today's value. See more
Supermajority
More than a simple majority, usually two thirds. Required here to issue units beyond the reserved pool. See more
Sweat equity
Ownership earned by working for the studio: wiring, buildout, engineering for clients, booking, cleaning. Logged weekly, approved monthly, valued at the rate for that kind of work, counted at 2x. See more
Tripwire
Any partner's right to call a review of studio-time usage, about the system or about one person's use of the room. See more
Units
The fixed pieces of ownership issued at the freeze. Your percentage is your units divided by all units issued. Easier to add a partner with than percentages. See more
W-9
The tax form a business hands over to get paid by another business. Having one in the LLC's name is part of being fundable. See more
Work
Hours for the studio logged at the rate for that kind of work, or a project priced up front. Not work on your own music. Exchange rate 2x. See sweat equity. See more
Worker
Someone paid to do a job for the studio without owning any of it: a contractor at first, an employee only once there's steady revenue. Not a partner, so no slices and no ledger line. See more
