Founding

Everything above is how the studio works once it exists. This part is how we get from here to there: one meeting, the building, and who does what afterward.

1Partner meeting: what we need to decide

Must decide at the meeting

1
Who's in. Partner (owns a piece, pays the flat monthly fee, votes) or regular (pays for time, no ownership). Default: anyone bringing cash, work, gear, or clients beyond the fee is a partner.
See Partner, Regular, Can you be two things?.
2
The monthly fee (see How is the monthly fee set?). Two numbers, then a choice. The floor: each person says what they can reliably pay every month, and the lowest reliable number is the floor. The ceiling: what unlimited access to a room like this is actually worth, what a regular or a lockout space would charge. Then pick where in that band to sit: near the ceiling means more deduction and less ownership; near the floor means more ownership and less deduction. Same fee for everyone. Whatever's left to cover rent comes from contributions.
See The fee, How is the monthly fee set?, What if not everyone can cover the same share of rent?.
3
What each person might bring beyond the fee. Nothing to commit to. Contributions are logged as they happen, and the pie follows. The only hard number anyone commits to is the monthly fee. This is a rough conversation so we can size the buildout budget and know whether the gap above the fees is covered. Here's what counts, for reference:
  • Cash: how much, and when. Counts at 4x.
  • Hours: roughly how many per week, and what kind of work (buildout, engineering, booking, cleaning). Counts at 2x at the rate for that work.
  • Business: clients, sponsorships, grants, deals, or funding you could bring in. 15% / 10% / 5% of what lands, as ownership or cash.
  • Lease guarantee: whether you'd co-sign. Valued at one to three months' rent, 2x.
  • Gear: list each item you'd bring, with one of three choices per item:
    • Sell it to the studio for ownership. The studio owns it from then on. You get slices once, at 2x its resale value today. If you leave, you can buy it back at that recorded value.
    • Lend it for free. You keep owning it, the studio uses it, no slices. It leaves with you whenever you want. The default for personal gear and anything under about $500.
    • Lend it for ownership. You keep owning it, and the studio's use earns you slices every month: 3% of resale value, at 2x. $5,000 of gear is 300 slices a month. Leaves with you if you go. For big shared pieces the studio depends on.
    The manager can say no to any item (space, condition, need). Resale value is agreed, not declared.

See What is a contribution, Cash, Work, Gear, Business, Risk.
4
Sunk costs. Proposal: everything before February 1 is a sunk cost, for everyone. The rent already paid, the hours already put in, the money already spent: none of it goes in the ledger, and the pie starts at zero on day one. The one exception is gear that's physically in the room, which goes through the normal gear options like anything a partner brings in. Objections?
See Sunk costs, Gear.
5
Start date. Proposal: everything starts February 1, 2027: the LLC, the lease, the membership, the ledger, the first fee. If the lease has to start earlier, there's a stopgap path for the gap that costs nobody anything. Objections?
See Everything starts in February 2027, If the lease has to start before February, The LLC.
6
Booking the studio. Fully open, calendar is king, nobody bumps anybody. Confirm.
See First to book has the room, The tripwire.
7
The reserve. How many months of rent the studio keeps in the bank before anything is distributed. Proposal: two to three. It's what makes a bad month survivable, and it's part of the freeze trigger, so the number matters.
See Where does the money go?, The freeze, What happens when the studio runs short?.
8
Rates and percentages. Walk the table in Money math once: work rates, gear rental, the three commission tiers, the guarantee value, the sign-off cap, the backfill window, the exchange rate. These are the numbers the operating agreement will carry, so every one needs a yes or a counter tonight.
See What are the rates and percentages?, Why is the exchange rate 4 and 2?, Dollar values.
9
Manager. Who, and manager pay (proposal: none for now; the manager's hours earn slices like anyone's). The role is a job, not an ownership level, and doesn't change anyone's share. Confirm.
See Manager, Voting, The manager can be replaced.

Defaults in this doc, change only if someone objects

All of these numbers are collected in Rates and percentages. Walk that table once; anything nobody objects to stands.

10
Exchange rate: cash 4x; work, gear, business, and risk 2x.
11
Work rates: $20 to $25, $30 to $40, $40 to $75 by kind of work.
12
Business commission: clients 15%, sponsorships/grants/deals 10%, loans/investment 5%, no cutoff. Partners choose ownership or cash per client; non-partners get cash.
13
Gear: lent for ownership earns 3% of resale value per month; partners borrow studio gear free, in the room and out of it; clients rent it only when it leaves the building (see Gear).
14
Voting: manager runs day to day; big decisions need a majority of units and at least two people; lease, sale, or dissolution needs everyone.
15
Leaving: anyone can leave with notice and keeps every slice. Before the freeze that's a claim on future profit; after it, a buyout at 1.5x annual profit times share, paid over 24 months. Removal for cause deducts actual damages only; "cause" is a short specific list.
16
Caps: cash contributions over $1,000 a month need manager sign-off (one other partner for the manager's own); repairs under $200 the manager just pays; gear for ownership over $2,000 needs a vote. Set a buildout budget (e.g. $5,000, first three months) that's pre-approved for anyone to fund.
17
Session pay: a partner who works a paid client session logs the hours for slices pre-freeze and is paid a guaranteed payment post-freeze; the client's money is the studio's (see Client sessions).
18
Regular fees: set by the manager on the fee sheet, usually above a partner's; a missed month pauses access (see Regular fees).

Logistics, five minutes

19
Bank. Which business account (no monthly fee, free Zelle or ACH). Everyone pays bank to bank.
20
Ledger and close date. A shared spreadsheet everyone can see. Decide the monthly close date: a fixed day between the 5th and the 10th, and which weeknight.
21
Lawyer and accountant. Who finds them, rough budget. Any New Jersey business lawyer willing to write dynamic equity into an operating agreement; having seen Slicing Pie before is a plus, not a requirement. Avoid anyone who says "just pick percentages" before reading this.
22
Insurance. Who gets quotes for general liability and property coverage; the value above which gear is scheduled individually; the certificate goes to the landlord before signing (see Liability).
23
February paperwork. Operating agreement signed, insurance bound, fee sheet written, and first invoices dated in February, so the studio is open for business on February 1.

After the meeting

Each partner
Nothing owed. Start logging contributions as they happen. For any gear you bring, pick an option per item with the manager when it comes in.
Manager
Updates this doc with what was decided, opens the bank account, starts the ledger, gets the insurance quotes, and lines up the lawyer and accountant so nothing in February is waiting on anyone.

2The Sterling

The building we're trying to get. What we know, what we don't, and how the lease would actually come to us.

Where it stands

What
Rent is about $2,000 a month. There's an existing tenant on the lease. The goal is 24/7 access as a commercial studio with partners and outside clients.
Why
Everything in this doc is sized to that rent and that use. If either changes, the monthly fee and the timing section change with it.
How
The manager is handling the conversation with the current tenant and the landlord. Partners will get the lease to read before anyone signs.
Specifics
Still unknown, to be filled in:
  • How the lease transfers (below)
  • Remaining term and rent escalations
  • Deposit amount and who holds the current one
  • Whether 24-hour access, hourly clients, and partners and regulars on a flat fee are permitted
  • Noise and zoning
  • What's in the room now and who owns it
  • Whether the landlord requires a personal guarantee, and from whom

Getting the lease: it's currently someone else's

What
The Sterling is leased by a current tenant who'd be handing it over. Details aren't known yet. There are three ways a lease changes hands, and they're very different for the studio.
Why
The whole structure assumes the LLC is the tenant with the landlord. Only two of the three paths get there, and one of them means inheriting a contract we haven't read.
How
  • A new lease. The landlord ends the current tenant's lease and signs a fresh one with the LLC. Cleanest: our terms, our start date, our deposit, nothing inherited. This is what to push for.
  • An assignment. The current tenant hands their existing lease to the LLC, with the landlord's written consent. The LLC steps into that lease as-is: their rent, remaining term, escalations, obligations. Fine if the lease is good. Read every page first, because we're taking whatever's in it.
  • A sublease. The current tenant stays on the lease and rents the space to the LLC. Worst option. We're only as secure as they are: if they stop paying the landlord, we can be evicted even though we paid them. Most leases also require landlord consent to sublet. Avoid unless it's the only path, and then only short-term with a plan to convert.
Specifics
Questions for the current tenant, in order:
  1. Which of the three is this, and does the landlord know?
  2. Can we see the lease? All of it, including amendments.
  3. How long is left on the term, and what does rent do over it?
  4. What happens to their security deposit, and is anything owed or damaged that we'd inherit?
  5. Who owns the buildout and anything left in the room?
If the answer to the first question is "not sure," the next conversation is with the landlord, not the tenant. The landlord decides whether the LLC becomes the tenant.

3Timing

The proposed start date is February 1, 2027. If the lease has to start earlier, there's a side path for the gap; everything else in this document assumes February.

Everything starts in February 2027

The LLC, the lease, the membership, the ledger, and the first fee all begin the same month.

What
The LLC is formed in the last week of January with every partner as a legal member from the first day, so that everything else can start on February 1. The lease is signed in the LLC's name, the bank account opens, the first monthly fees are invoiced, and the ledger starts logging contributions. One start date for everything.
Why
A 2027 start means no 2026 filings at all: no partnership return, no New Jersey per-owner fee, nothing for the accountant to untangle. 2027 is one clean return, and every partner's name is on the paperwork from day one instead of being admitted later. February rather than January gives the lawyer and the insurance quotes room to land.
How
  • Now: the partner meeting. Everything else waits on it: the lawyer can't draft until we've agreed, and the insurer can't quote until there's a gear list and an address.
  • December: hand this manual to the lawyer and ask for a draft; get insurance quotes on the address and the gear list; confirm the lease path with the landlord.
  • January: the lawyer's draft reviewed and finalized; the lease read by the lawyer. In the last week, file the formation with every partner as a member, get the tax ID the same day, register with the state, and open the bank account, so the account exists before any money moves. On the bank's ownership form the manager is the control person and, with the pie at zero, the only name (see Dates for when the bank asks again).
  • February 1: bind insurance, sign the operating agreement, sign the lease as the LLC, invoice the first fees, start the ledger.
  • Open fast. Everything a business spends before it opens is a "startup cost," and only $5,000 of that is deductible in the first year; the rest trickles out over 15 years. Everything spent after opening is a normal expense. "Open" means available to customers: a dated fee sheet, a way to book, and a room that can record something. Keep dated copies; that's the opening date if anyone asks.
Rules
  • Forming the LLC, signing the lease, and buying gear are preparation, not opening. Partners paying for February access is.
  • The first K-1 covers 2027 and arrives by mid-March 2028. Nothing changes about anyone's 2026 taxes.
  • Everything before the start date is a sunk cost (agenda item 4). The ledger's first line is dated February 1.

If the lease has to start before February

Optional. A way not to waste the gap if rent starts in December or January.

What
The manager's existing consulting LLC takes the lease when it starts, the studio opens right away, and the studio LLC with every partner takes over on February 1. Nothing else about the plan changes.
Why
Forming the studio LLC early with everyone in it would mean a full partnership return for 2026 even for three weeks, plus the per-owner fee: roughly $2,000 of filings for one month. The consulting LLC already files, so the gap months just ride on it. Opening early also turns rent, insurance, and setup in those months into normal deductions on the manager's own return instead of capped startup costs.
How
  • The stopgap tenant. The consulting LLC already exists and can sign tomorrow. The lease says, in writing, that it will be assigned to the studio LLC on February 1 with the landlord's consent already given.
  • February 1, the handoff. The lease is assigned to the studio LLC, the deposit transfers, the gear in the room goes on the gear list, and the consulting LLC is out of it.
  • Partners pay their fee for those months as customers, invoiced and dated in the month. They get their deduction; the studio gets income on the books. That's also the strongest evidence the studio is open.
  • Gear that comes in during the gap goes through the normal gear options on February 1, like anything else in the room. Rent, hours, and money in the gap are sunk, same as before it.
  • The manager signs a letter listing what's in the room and who owns it on February 1, so nothing rests on memory.
Rules
  • Only worth doing if rent actually starts before February. If the lease starts in February, skip this card.
  • The consulting LLC is a stopgap only. It holds the lease and collects the gap months' fees, and nothing more: no partners join it, no studio gear is bought in its name, and the lease leaves it on February 1. That keeps its history and its consulting income out of the pie.
  • The legal membership starts on February 1 for everyone. Nobody loses anything by the ordering.
  • Any loss in the gap lands on the manager's own return (Schedule C), through the consulting LLC. Security deposit isn't deductible; rent is.

What each partner supplies

The manager does the filings. This is the partner's list, and it's short.

In December: a written list of the gear you're bringing, each item with a resale value and a couple of comps from Reverb or eBay sold listings, so the manager can get the insurance quote and agree the values.

By late January: a W-9 with your legal name, address, and Social Security number, so the K-1 goes to the right person. Your email for the ledger, the calendar, and the shared folder. Your bank set up for Zelle or bill pay to the LLC.

On signing day: a government ID and your signature on the operating agreement. New Jersey doesn't require it to be notarized; the lawyer may want signatures witnessed anyway, which is five minutes in their office or a video call.

If you own 25% or more on the day the bank asks, usually at its yearly review, the manager will ask you for a copy of your ID and your address to send in. Crossing 25% at a monthly close triggers nothing by itself. Banks have to identify large owners; it's a bank record, nothing more (see Dates).

If you sign the lease guarantee, the landlord may ask for a credit check or a financial statement, and may want that signature notarized. That's the only notary likely to appear.

Nothing else. No state registration for partners, no estimated taxes pre-freeze, no 83(b) election, since no units are issued until the freeze. Your next piece of paperwork after signing is the K-1 in March 2028.

Gear can be bought any year

What
Federally, gear is fully written off in the year it's placed in service, every year. No one-time window.
Why
A February start doesn't force spending into any particular year.
How
Buy when the studio needs it.
Rules
New Jersey caps immediate write-offs at $25,000 per year. Big purchases split across two calendar years get two state caps.

Glossary

Every term this document leans on, alphabetical. Each links to where it's actually used.

83(b) election
A form filed with the IRS within 30 days of receiving ownership for work, so it's taxed now at its low value instead of later. No extensions. See more
Active partner
A partner who does real work for the business, not just records in it. Active partners can deduct their share of losses against other income federally. See more
Assignment / sublease / new lease
The three ways a lease can come to us from the current tenant. New lease is cleanest; assignment means inheriting their lease as-is; sublease means they stay on the hook and we're only as secure as they are. See more
Basis
Each partner's tax record of what they've put in. Contributions raise it; it's not income and not a deduction. See more
Books
The studio's bookkeeping: every dollar in and out of the bank account, fees, rent, bills, distributions. Kept for the accountant and for taxes. Not the ledger, which tracks contributions and ownership. The two meet only at cash contributions, which appear in both. See more
Breakeven
The point where the studio's outside income covers rent and bills, keeps the reserve full, and can pay fair rates for all the work it still needs. Not the same as being slightly profitable. See more
Business
Outside money you bring to the studio: clients (15% of what they pay), sponsorships, grants, and deals (10% of what lands), or loans and investment (5% of what lands). Counted when the money arrives, as slices or as a cash finder's fee. Exchange rate 2x. See more
Buyout
How a departing partner's ownership is bought back after the freeze: 1.5x annual profit times their share, paid over 24 months, capped so it can't starve the rent. See more
Calendar is king
The studio-time rule: first to book has the room, partner or client, and nobody bumps anybody. See more
Capital call
A request for partners to put in money when the studio genuinely runs short. Split by ownership share; the cash counts as a contribution. Unpaid after 90 days triggers dilution. See more
Cash
Money put into the LLC, or an expense you paid personally and didn't get reimbursed for. Counted at face value, exchange rate 4x. The monthly fee is not cash in this sense; it's a payment for the room. See more
Cause
The short, specific list of things that justify removing a partner: theft or fraud, a capital call unpaid 90+ days after notice, conduct that gets the studio evicted or uninsurable. Using the room a lot is not cause. See more
Certificate of insurance
Proof of the studio's insurance that venues and other businesses ask for before letting us load in or work with them. See more
Claim on future profit
What a partner who leaves before breakeven keeps: their earned slices as a non-voting stake that pays out only if the studio becomes profitable. Not a cash payout at the door. See more
Client
Books the room by the hour or per session. See more
Commission
15% of what a client pays the studio, earned by whoever brought the client in. A partner takes it as slices or as cash; a regular takes cash. See more
Contribution
Anything given to the company beyond the monthly fee: cash, unreimbursed expenses, work, gear, clients, the lease guarantee. Every contribution earns slices. See more
Contribution type
Which of the five kinds a contribution is: cash, work, gear, business, or risk. The type decides how the contribution gets its dollar value and which exchange rate applies. See more
Dilution
When new units are issued and everyone else's percentage goes down even though their unit count didn't. Fair when the new person brought real value; unfair when they didn't. See more
EIN
Employer Identification Number: the LLC's federal tax ID. Free from the IRS, needed to open the bank account. See more
Exchange rate
What the multipliers amount to: one dollar of cash equals two dollars of work. Set by vote before anyone contributes; a later change applies going forward only. See more
Fee
A payment for use of the room. The monthly fee partners pay, or what a client pays per hour. Fees are income to the studio and deductible for the payer. They never earn slices. See more
Freeze
What happens once the studio can pay its own way: the ownership calculation stops, everyone's percentage becomes fixed, and units are issued to match. After the freeze, work is paid in money. See more
Gear
Equipment you own, either sold to the LLC for ownership (resale value, once) or lent to it for ownership (3% of resale value every month it's in the room). Lending for free is also allowed and earns nothing. Exchange rate 2x. See more
Good leaver / bad leaver
A distinction this agreement mostly doesn't make. Everyone who leaves keeps every slice they earned. The only difference is removal for cause, where actual damages are deducted from the claim or buyout. See more
K-1
The yearly tax form the LLC sends each partner showing their share of profit or loss. See more
Lease guarantee
A personal promise to the landlord to cover the rent if the LLC can't. Whoever signs it is taking real risk, so it counts as a contribution, valued at one to three months' rent. See more
Ledger
The shared record of every contribution: date, who, what, value, slices. The whole system depends on it being kept. See more
Limited liability
The legal protection an LLC gives its owners: the company's debts and lawsuits can't reach personal savings, as long as the company is run as a real company and nobody personally guaranteed the debt. See more
LLC
Limited liability company. The legal entity that is the studio: it signs the lease, owns the gear, holds the bank account, and keeps its problems away from anyone's personal savings. See more
Majority of units and two people
The voting rule for big decisions: more than half the ownership and at least two partners must agree. Stops one big owner from acting alone and one small owner from blocking alone. See more
Manager
The partner who runs day to day: bills, approvals, the landlord, the monthly close. A job, not an ownership level. Can be replaced by vote. See more
Manager-managed
An LLC where one named person runs operations and the owners vote only on listed big decisions. The alternative, member-managed, means everyone votes on everything. See more
Mediation / arbitration
The two steps before court for a deadlock: a neutral third party helps you agree (non-binding), then a private arbitrator decides (binding). Faster and cheaper than a lawsuit. See more
Member
New Jersey's legal word for an owner of an LLC. In this document it only ever means that. A partner is a member; a regular is not. See more
Membership interest
The legal term for owning part of an LLC, in New Jersey law and in the operating agreement. What this document calls ownership or equity, and what becomes units at the freeze. Expect the lawyer's draft to say this. See more
Monthly close
The manager's monthly routine, about an hour: reconcile the bank, tag every deposit, approve hours, update the pie, post the split. See more
Monthly fee
The flat amount every partner pays each month for unlimited use of the room. Set in a band between what everyone can reliably pay and what the access is worth, same for everyone, changed only by vote. It is not a contribution: it buys use of the room, earns no slices, and is deductible for the payer. Anything paid above it counts as a contribution unless you say otherwise. See more
Multiplier
The weight applied to a contribution's dollar value to turn it into slices: 4x for cash, 2x for work, gear, business, and the lease guarantee. It sets the exchange rate between cash and everything else. See more
Open for business
The point where the studio is actually available to customers: a fee sheet, a way to book, a usable room, someone paying. Turns spending from startup costs into normal expenses. See more
Operating agreement
The contract between the owners that sets the rules: ownership, money, votes, leaving. A lawyer writes it from this document. Without one, the state's defaults apply. See more
Ownership
Your percentage of the company. Pre-freeze it's your slices out of all slices that exist, and it moves monthly. Post-freeze it's your units out of all units, and it's fixed. Same thing as equity; the legal term is membership interest. See more
Partner
Owns a piece of the studio. Pays the flat monthly fee, earns ownership for what they put in beyond it, votes on big decisions. Legally, an LLC member. Out in the world: a co-owner. See more
Pass-through
How an LLC is taxed by default: the company pays no income tax itself; profit or loss passes to each owner's personal return. See more
Permitted use
The lease clause saying what the space may be used for. Ours must say recording studio, with 24-hour access and public sessions, in writing. See more
Per-owner fee
New Jersey's $150 per owner per year on partnerships with more than two owners. The studio pays it, not the partners. See more
Post-freeze
The second phase. The studio's income covers rent, bills, the reserve, and fair pay for its work, so the calculation stops: percentages become units and stay fixed, work is paid in money, and ownership changes only by vote. See more
Pre-emptive rights
When new units are issued, existing partners get first crack at buying their share, so anyone who wants to hold their percentage can. See more
Pre-freeze
The first phase. The studio can't yet pay for the work it needs, so it pays in ownership: contributions become slices, and percentages move every month. All of the Ownership model section is pre-freeze rules. See more
Profits interest
A form of ownership granted for work that's taxed on future growth rather than on the day it's received. The plan avoids needing it by not issuing units until the freeze. See more
Project pricing
Valuing a piece of work with a finish line as a fixed amount agreed up front, like a contractor's quote, instead of logging hours. Slices on completion. See more
Regular
Uses the room on a flat monthly fee but owns none of it. No slices, no vote. Can bring in clients for a cash commission. See more
Reserve
Cash the studio keeps on hand, target two to three months of rent, before anything is distributed. What makes a bad month survivable. See more
Reserved pool
Units set aside at the freeze for future partners, so bringing someone in doesn't dilute anyone until the pool is used up. See more
Risk
Signing the lease guarantee: personally promising the landlord the rent if the LLC can't pay. Valued at one to three months of rent, agreed up front. Exchange rate 2x. See more
Section 179
The federal rule letting a business write off gear in full the year it's bought. New Jersey caps it at $25,000 a year. See more
Self-employment tax
Roughly 15% owed by active partners on their share of profit, on top of income tax. See more
Shotgun clause
A deadlock breaker between two partners: one names a price per unit, the other must buy or sell at it. Forces an honest number. Optional. See more
Slice
Earned through contributions in the form of cash, work, gear, business, and risk. A slice represents what you've put into the studio, measured in one unit so those five things can be compared. Slices aren't money and can't be cashed out; they only mean something relative to everyone else's. Your ownership percentage is your slices out of all slices that exist, and it moves every month. Slices only go up while you're a partner. See more
Slicing Pie
A published model for splitting a company by what people actually put in, instead of negotiating percentages up front. Everything in the Ownership model section runs on it. See more
Startup costs
Everything spent before the business opens. Only $5,000 is deductible in the first year; the rest trickles out over 15 years. Everything after opening day is a normal expense. See more
Sunk costs
Money or work spent before the LLC existed on things the studio never receives: an old space, lessons, your own records, gear that didn't come with you. Not a contribution, however much it was. Gear that's physically in the room on the start date is the one exception; it goes through the normal gear options at today's value. See more
Supermajority
More than a simple majority, usually two thirds. Required here to issue units beyond the reserved pool. See more
Sweat equity
Ownership earned by working for the studio: wiring, buildout, engineering for clients, booking, cleaning. Logged weekly, approved monthly, valued at the rate for that kind of work, counted at 2x. See more
Tripwire
Any partner's right to call a review of studio-time usage, about the system or about one person's use of the room. See more
Units
The fixed pieces of ownership issued at the freeze. Your percentage is your units divided by all units issued. Easier to add a partner with than percentages. See more
W-9
The tax form a business hands over to get paid by another business. Having one in the LLC's name is part of being fundable. See more
Work
Hours for the studio logged at the rate for that kind of work, or a project priced up front. Not work on your own music. Exchange rate 2x. See sweat equity. See more
Worker
Someone paid to do a job for the studio without owning any of it: a contractor at first, an employee only once there's steady revenue. Not a partner, so no slices and no ledger line. See more
