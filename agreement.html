The Agreement Manual

Every word in here needs to be something we all agree to.

What the studio is, who's in it, and how ownership, contributions, taxes, money, decisions, and leaving actually work.

This manual is what we agree to. We use it to create the Operating Agreement, which a lawyer reviews before we sign. Nothing goes in there that isn't in here.

1Why do we need this?

Because if we don't write our own rules, New Jersey writes them for us.

What a company gives us

Limited liability. When something goes wrong, a lawsuit, unpaid rent, a wrecked console, the company is on the hook and personal savings aren't, as long as the company is run as a real company.

Continuity. Partners can join and leave; the LLC stays the tenant, the owner, and the account holder. Records, shows, tours, and gear stop coming out of somebody's pocket with no record and no way back. There's one account and one set of books: deposits come back, door money splits clean, and every dollar in or out is on paper that outlives whoever paid it. Grants, sponsors, loans, investors, and label advances have something to write a check to, and something that will still be there to pay it back.

Standing. Landlords, publishers, distributors, labels, banks, venues, studios, record stores, gear shops, collectives, festivals, sponsors, and grant programs deal with a company, not a group chat. Every real partnership is a contract; the LLC signs it, holds the insurance certificate, and hands over the W-9, so nobody is signing personally every time.

A better tax situation. The studio's rent, insurance, and gear become the partners' federal deduction instead of one person's, split by ownership, and ownership tracks what each partner put in. Put in more, own more, deduct more. The fee is a clean business expense on top, and once there's profit the structure can lower the tax on it (see Taxes).

2We own the company

The studio is a New Jersey LLC. It's the thing that signs, owns, pays, and gets sued, so that none of us has to.

The LLC

One legal person standing in for all of us.

What it is
A separate legal person. Registered with the State of New Jersey, distinct from every partner. Partners own it; they aren't it.
It holds everything. The lease, the gear, the bank account. It invoices clients and pays the bills.
Manager-managed. One partner runs day to day; the owners vote on the big things.
Taxed as a partnership. The company itself pays no income tax; each partner reports their ownership share of its profit or loss on their own return (see Taxes).
What it isn't
Not a person's business with friends attached. Not a corporation with stock. Not a brand name on a handshake. If it isn't in the LLC's name, the LLC doesn't own it and isn't responsible for it.
How

Forming it. A state filing, a tax ID, a bank account, and insurance, done in late January 2027 with every partner a member from day one, so the studio can start February 1 (see Timing).

Running it. Every dollar in and out goes through the LLC's account. Every contract, lease, and invoice is in the LLC's name. The operating agreement, drafted from this manual, is the rulebook; where it's silent, New Jersey's defaults apply, which is why it shouldn't be silent.

Rules
  • Nobody pays the landlord or a vendor directly, ever. Money goes to the LLC's account and the LLC pays (see How does money actually move?).
  • No mixing money. No personal expenses from the studio account, and nothing the studio uses bought on a personal card unless it's logged in the ledger as a reimbursement or a cash or gear contribution. This is what keeps limited liability standing.
  • Every partner is a legal member of the LLC from the day they're admitted (see Partner). No side promises, no "we'll paper it later."
  • The LLC is the tenant. The lease is in the company's name, never a partner's (see below).
  • The LLC is insured. General liability and gear coverage in the company's name, with a current certificate of insurance on file (see Next steps).
  • The manager runs day to day; the owners vote on the big things. What needs a vote is listed under Voting.
  • The annual report and the state's per-owner fee are the studio's bills, paid from the account, never passed to partners.

The Agreement

A separate legal document, not this one.

What it is. The operating agreement: the contract between the owners of the LLC, written from the first part of this manual, reviewed by a lawyer, signed by every partner, and binding under New Jersey law. It lives in a folder, not on this page.

What this manual's first part is. The spec for it, in plain language: what the company is, who owns it, how contributions become ownership, how money and taxes work, how decisions get made, and what happens when someone leaves. We agree to it here first; the lawyer turns it into clauses. Nothing goes in the Agreement that isn't in here.

How hard it is to change. A rule in the Agreement binds. Changing one means a vote at the threshold the rule itself sets, this manual updated to match, and the lawyer amending the Agreement.

Where it overlaps the operating manual, the Agreement wins, and the manual's version is just the procedure for following it. The monthly close is the clearest example: the Agreement says contributions convert to slices on a fixed date; the manual says what the manager does that day.

The Studio Operating Manual

The second part of this document. How the room actually runs. Never part of the Agreement.

What's in it. Booking, gear, moving money, and the monthly close. House rules, and instructions for the manager.

How hard it is to change. A regular vote and an edit here. It has to stay consistent with the Agreement, but no lawyer is involved.

Commercial space

A real commercial lease, in the company's name, for exactly what we're doing.

What it is
The LLC is the tenant. The landlord's contract is with the company: the company owes the rent, has the right to be there, and is who the landlord deals with.
Commercial, 24/7. A space leased for a recording studio open to the public, with partners in the room on a flat fee and clients booking the rest (see Booking the studio).
What it isn't
Not a lease in one partner's name with the others as guests. Not a sublease from a partner to the studio. Not a rehearsal room we quietly run sessions out of. Either of the first two makes one person the studio, which is what this manual exists to avoid; the third gets us evicted.
Why
So the studio's obligations are the studio's, and so the space survives any partner leaving. A landlord will almost certainly want a personal guarantee from someone on a brand-new company; whoever signs it is taking real risk, and that's the risk contribution, valued at one to three months of rent.
How
The manager signs as manager of the LLC, not as themselves. The guarantee is signed by the manager alone or by every partner in equal shares (the pie is at zero on signing day), decided by vote before the lease. Deposit and first month come from the LLC's account. The certificate of insurance goes to the landlord before signing. Target start is February 2027.
The lease
Before anyone signs, the lease is checked for these, in writing. A must-have missing is a no; a nice-to-have missing is a negotiation.
  • Permitted use is a recording studio, including sessions for the public and partners and regulars using the room on a flat fee. Many commercial leases prohibit this by default. (Must have)
  • 24-hour access, and the noise rules we're agreeing to live under. (Must have)
  • Zoning allows commercial studio use at the address. (Must have)
  • Adding or removing members of the LLC isn't an assignment and doesn't need the landlord's consent. (Must have)
  • A renewal option, so the studio isn't month to month at the landlord's mood when the term ends. (Nice to have)
  • Who owns the buildout and anything left in the room when the lease ends. (Nice to have)
Signing the lease, or ending it, is one of the few things that needs everyone's vote. A lawyer reads it first. It's the biggest contract the studio will ever have.

3Company roles

From the people who book the room to the people who run it.

Who's who

  • Client. Books the room by the hour or per session.
  • Regular. Uses the room on a flat monthly fee but doesn't own any of it. No slices, no vote. Can bring in clients for a cash commission.
  • Partner (you, most likely). Owns a piece of the studio. Pays the flat monthly fee, earns slices for what they put in beyond it, votes on big decisions. In New Jersey's legal language a partner is an LLC "member," and that's the only sense this document uses "member" in.
  • Manager. The partner who runs day to day. A job, not an ownership level.
  • Worker. Paid to do a job for the studio without owning any of it: a contractor at first, an employee only once there's steady revenue. None at the start.

Out in the world, partners are co-owners of the studio. "I'm a partner at [the studio]" or "I co-own a studio" are both accurate. The manager can also say "I run it." Nobody needs to say "member" to anyone but a lawyer or the state.

See more

Can you be two things?

  • Partner and manager: yes. The manager has to be a partner. It's a job on top of ownership.
  • Partner and regular: no. A regular is what a partner would be without the ownership. The fee is the same idea; the slices are the difference.
  • Partner and client: only in one case. A partner's own use is covered by the fee, so they never book by the hour for themselves. But a band with non-partners in it, or a project a partner is producing for someone else, books as a client and pays like one. The partner is representing the client, not acting as a partner.
  • Partner and worker: no, pre-freeze. A partner's work earns slices, never pay. Post-freeze, partners who work get paid, but as partners, through guaranteed payments, not as employees, until an S-corp election puts working partners on payroll (see Electing S-corp).

Manager

What
The LLC is "manager-managed." One partner handles day-to-day decisions: paying bills, approving hours, buying consumables, talking to the landlord. The role has nothing to do with how much of the studio they own. It carries no extra votes, no founder premium, and no bigger profit share.
Why
A studio with four owners voting on every cable purchase doesn't function. Partners own the upside and vote on the big stuff; the manager keeps the lights on.
How
The operating agreement names the manager and lists exactly which decisions the manager can't make alone (see Voting).
Rules
  • Manager is a job, not an ownership level. The agreement names the manager by name. Their percentage can be 60% or 15%; it doesn't change what the role can do. The title gives no extra votes, no founder premium, and no bigger profit share.
  • The manager's hours (booking, landlord, the monthly close) earn slices at the rate for that kind of work, like anyone's. That's the only way the role affects ownership.
  • The manager can be replaced by vote: majority of units and at least two people, not counting the manager's own units (see Decisions and disputes).
  • If the manager draws any pay for the role, the amount is written in the agreement and can't be raised without a vote.

Workers

What
Anyone paid to do work for the studio who doesn't own a piece of it: a session engineer brought in for a client, someone who cleans weekly, front desk help. There are none at the start. The pie phase is partners paid in slices.
Contractor
Paid per job or per hour on a 1099. Only if they're genuinely independent: their own hours, their own gear, other clients. The first non-partner workers should be contractors.
Employee
Anyone working set hours under the studio's direction. New Jersey treats them as an employee whether or not you call them one, which means payroll, workers' comp, and unemployment insurance. Not until there's steady revenue to cover it.
Rules
A partner can't be an employee of their own partnership. When the studio pays a partner for work after the freeze, it's a guaranteed payment on their K-1, not a salary. Regulars are people who use the room, not people who work in it.

4Ownership model

How ownership of the studio is decided.

Slicing Pie

Fixed percentages agreed to on day one are never an accurate representation of what's really going on.

Slicing Pie is a proven two-phase model that tracks ownership as a function of contributions instead. It's Mike Moyer's, in use since 2012, and nothing about it is proprietary: it's rules from a book, any business lawyer can write it into an operating agreement, and the ledger can be a spreadsheet.

Pre-freeze, the studio can't yet pay for its work, so everything is paid in ownership.

Post-freeze, it can, and it runs like an ordinary company.

Contributions in, ownership out. A contribution gets a dollar value, the exchange rate turns that into slices, and the percentage of slices you have out of the total is the percentage of the company you own (ownership).

This makes sure everyone gets their fair share of whatever the studio becomes: the profit, the proceeds, the say in running it, the losses. Whether what they put in was cash, work, gear, business, or risk.

Slices

The unit ownership is counted in before the freeze.

What it is
A running count of what a partner has put into the studio, in one unit so cash, work, gear, business, and risk can be compared. Every contribution becomes slices through the exchange rate. A partner's ownership percentage is their slices divided by all slices that exist.
What it isn't
Not money. Slices can't be cashed out, and a slice count means nothing on its own; 9,000 slices is a lot or a little depending on the total. Not units: slices move every month, units are fixed and only exist after the freeze.
Rules
Slices only go up while someone is a partner. They come off in exactly three cases: a contribution is returned, a ledger error is corrected within three months, or a removal for cause deducts actual damages. Nobody forfeits slices for leaving. At the freeze, slices convert to units at each partner's exact percentage.

Pre-freeze

What
The studio can't pay anyone, so it pays in ownership. Instead of people deciding on an arbitrary percentage up front that feels good, cash, work, gear, business, or risk that a partner contributes gets converted into slices. The number of slices you have only ever goes up as you contribute more while you're a partner. The percentage of slices you have out of the total amount of slices that exist reflects your ownership percentage, and it moves every month as contributions continue to happen.
How
Every contribution goes into a ledger and, using an exchange rate, gets converted into something called slices, which represent ownership. Your slices divided by all slices is your share, recalculated at every monthly close. See more

What a changing percentage actually means

What
Nothing happens day to day when your share moves. Nobody writes a check. Your percentage only matters at the moments money or power actually moves.
Why
Ownership is a claim on what the studio is worth and what it earns. It's not cash.
How
  • Profit distributions: split by the pie as it stands that day.
  • Votes: weighted by the pie, where the agreement says ownership votes.
  • Someone leaves: their slices that day set what they're owed.
  • A sale: the pie that day is who gets what.
Specifics
During the early phase, the pie is a spreadsheet everyone has agreed to honor when it counts. The ledger is the whole mechanism.

The freeze

What
When the studio can pay people for their work instead of giving ownership for it, the calculation stops. Whatever the split is that month becomes the fixed split, and real units are issued to match.
Why
Dynamic equity is for the risky early phase. Once the business is self-sustaining, people get paid in money and the ownership stops moving.
How
The freeze is not "the studio made a profit." It's "the studio no longer needs to pay anyone in slices." Proposed trigger: for three consecutive months, outside income covers rent and bills, keeps the reserve full, and pays fair rates for all the work the studio still needs. The manager calls it; partners confirm by vote.
  • Being slightly in the black doesn't freeze anything. $400 a month left after rent, with the builder still doing 60 hours, means the studio can't pay him. His hours keep earning slices.
  • The reserve fills first. Surplus goes to the two-to-three-month rent cushion before anyone is paid for work.
  • Then partial payment. Once the reserve is there, the studio can pay some work in cash. If it pays $400 of a $3,000 month, the other $2,600 still earns slices at 2x. Only the unpaid part counts.
  • The pie winds down on its own. As income grows, more work is paid in cash and less is sliced. The freeze is the month nothing needs slicing anymore.
Rules
  • After the freeze, work is paid in money, client referrals earn a cash commission, and ownership only changes if the partners deliberately vote to issue new units to someone. A reserved pool (10 to 15%) is set aside at that point for future partners.
  • It doesn't unfreeze on its own. A bad month after the freeze doesn't reopen the pie. If it did, nobody's percentage would ever really be fixed.
  • Short dip: loans and deferred pay. Before the freeze, cash covering a bad month is a contribution at 4x like any cash. After the freeze, a partner covering a gap is lending the studio money: it's paid back first out of the next surplus, before any distributions, and no ownership moves. Same for work: the studio keeps owing the agreed rate, just pays late. The contribution counts; it's just repaid in money, because after the freeze the studio can.
  • Real downturn: reopen by vote. If the covering keeps happening (three months or more, or past a set amount), it's not a bad month, it's a downturn. The partners vote to reopen the pie for new contributions only, bounded by a date or an amount. Cash and work put in during that window earn units from the reserved pool, same multipliers, same ledger. The frozen split stays as everyone's baseline.

Post-freeze

What
When the studio's own income covers rent, bills, the reserve, and fair pay for the work it still needs, the pie freezes: no more slices are earned, and whatever percentage each partner holds that month becomes permanent, written down as units: a fixed count whose share of all units issued is your ownership. From then on the studio is an ordinary company.
Why
Dynamic equity is for the risky phase. Once the business can pay people in money, it should, and ownership should stop moving so everyone knows what they hold.
How
Work is paid in money. Referrals earn a cash fee. Cash put in is a loan or a priced buy-in. New partners get units from the reserved pool, by vote. Nobody's unit count changes unless the partners vote to change it.
Rules
The trigger is being able to pay for the work. It doesn't unfreeze on its own either. Details in The freeze. Most of Money math, Leaving the company, and Taxes matter more in this phase.

Units

What
Ownership is always a percentage. Units are how that percentage gets written down once it's fixed. At the freeze, the LLC issues a set number of units, say 10,000, split to match everyone's slices, and from then on your percentage is your units divided by all units issued.
Why
Pre-freeze, the percentage moves every month, so there's nothing to write down. Post-freeze it's permanent, and the operating agreement needs a fixed, legal form for it. Units also make adding a partner later simple: issue them some, and the math updates itself.
Rules
A block of units, 10 to 15%, is set aside at the freeze as the reserved pool for future partners. Before the freeze there are no units, only slices.

The reserved pool

What
A block of units, 10 to 15% of the total, set aside unissued on freeze day. Nobody owns them. They exist so the studio can bring in a new partner later without taking anything from the people already in.
Why
After the freeze, percentages are fixed. Without a pool, every new partner would mean issuing new units and shrinking everyone's share, which is the dilution fight. With a pool, the first new partners come out of units that were never anyone's.
How
  • It doesn't exist until the freeze. While the pie is running there are no units, so there's nothing to set aside. New partners before the freeze just start at zero slices and earn.
  • At the freeze, units are issued to match everyone's slices, and the pool is carved out on top. If 10,000 units are issued to partners, another 1,100 to 1,800 sit in the pool, so the pool is 10 to 15% of the total.
  • Using it is a regular vote: who the new partner is, how many pool units they get, and at what price or for what contribution.
  • When it's empty, adding a partner means issuing new units beyond it, which takes a supermajority and triggers pre-emptive rights.
Rules
Pool units carry no vote and no profit share while they sit there; the math treats them as not existing until issued. Not the same thing as the cash reserve, which is money in the bank for a bad month. One is units, the other is dollars.

Dilution

What
When new units are issued to someone, everyone else's percentage goes down, even though their unit count didn't change. The pie got bigger; your slice didn't.
Why
It's fair when the new person brings in value worth what they're getting (gear, cash, work). It's unfair when units get issued for nothing, or to the manager, without consent.
How
  • New partners come out of the reserved pool first. Nobody's percentage moves until it's empty.
  • Issuing units beyond the pool needs a supermajority vote.
  • Pre-emptive rights: when new units are issued, existing partners get first crack at buying their share of them, so anyone who wants to hold their percentage can.
Specifics
During the Slicing Pie phase, dilution is earned by definition: a new person only grows their share by contributing, which is the same rule everyone else lives by.

5Contributions

What partners put in, and how it's tracked.

What is a contribution

Being a partner means putting something in. There are five types of contribution: cash, work, gear, business, and risk. Each one gets a dollar value when it happens, goes into the ledger, and becomes ownership through the exchange rate in the next section. Each type has its own card below. One thing that is never a contribution: sunk costs, money or work spent before the LLC existed on things the studio never actually receives.

The Ledger

Where every contribution gets written down.

What it is
The ledger is one shared spreadsheet that keeps track of contributions, which every partner can access. It's a pre-freeze instrument: contribution lines are added and converted into slices until the freeze, and after that it stays as the record of how everyone got their units. Only partners have lines in it; regulars, workers, and clients don't. One line per contribution: date, which partner, type (cash, work, gear, business, risk), what it was, dollar value, and a status. The ownership calculation reads from it and nothing else.
What it isn't
Not the studio's bookkeeping; the bank account and the accountant's books track money in and out, and monthly fees never appear here. Not a log of who used the room; use isn't tracked. Not a to-do list or a place to claim things a partner plans to do. Not private.
Why
It's the whole system. Ownership is only as fair as the record it's computed from, and memory is the worst possible record. If it's in the ledger, it happened. If it isn't, it didn't.
How
  • Partners log their own, the week it happens. Cash when it lands. Expenses when the receipt is in hand. Hours the week they were worked. Projects when done, or at agreed milestones. Gear when the manager accepts it. Business: the partner claims the client at the first booking, and the manager adds the commission line at each close from what was actually paid. The guarantee the day the lease is signed.
  • The manager approves at the monthly close, checking each line against the bank, the receipt, the calendar, or the work, and asking the person who logged it when that isn't enough. Each line ends the close with one of the statuses below.
  • Every partner sees everything, the ledger and the current split, at all times. Each partner checks their own lines monthly.

Contribution types

  • Cash. Money a partner puts into the LLC, or an expense they paid and didn't get back.
  • Work. Hours on the business, or a project priced up front.
  • Gear. Equipment a partner sells or lends to the studio for ownership.
  • Business. Clients, deals, sponsorships, or funding a partner brings in.
  • Risk. Signing the lease guarantee.

The type decides how a contribution gets its dollar value and which exchange rate applies to it.

Cash

Money into the company.

What it is
Money a partner puts into the LLC for anything it needs: gear, buildout, a capital call, covering a gap. Also expenses a partner paid personally for the studio and chose not to be reimbursed for.
What it isn't
The monthly fee, which buys use of the room. Expenses the studio paid the partner back for. A loan the partner expects repaid. Money promised but not yet in the account. Anything spent before the start date, which is a sunk cost for everyone: past rent, an old space, lessons, a partner's own records.
Value
Face value, or the receipt amount.
How

Putting money in. The partner transfers it from their bank to the LLC's account, Zelle or ACH, with "contribution" and what it's for in the memo. No cash apps, no handing the manager a stack. Over $1,000 in a month, they get the sign-off first (see Rules).

Paying for something personally. The partner buys it, drops the receipt in the shared folder, and marks it "contribution." If they'd rather be paid back, they mark it "reimburse" instead; it can't be both.

Logging it. That same week, the partner adds a line to the ledger: date, amount, what it was for, and "cash." The manager matches it to the bank statement or receipt at the monthly close.

Rules
  • Anything paid above the monthly fee is a contribution unless the partner says otherwise.
  • Over $1,000 in a month needs the manager's sign-off first, so nobody buys the studio in one check. If the studio actually needs the money (treatment, construction, a big gear purchase, a capital call) the answer is yes and the slices count in full; if it's just to buy share, it waits or gets spread over months. The manager's own needs another partner's okay; a message saying "yes" is enough.
  • A pre-approved buildout budget covers spending inside it. Set at the partner meeting (say $5,000 for treatment, construction, and wiring in the first three months); no per-purchase sign-off, receipts in the folder, line in the ledger. The cap itself can be raised by vote for a specific purpose.
  • It goes through the LLC's account. No paying the landlord or a vendor directly on the studio's behalf and calling it a contribution; the money or the receipt has to land where the ledger can see it.
  • A contribution can't be taken back. If a partner wants the money returned, that's a loan: say so before it lands, it earns no slices, and the studio repays it when it can.
  • Cash for new gear counts here, not under Gear. The LLC buys the gear; the partner contributed the cash.

Work

Sweat equity. Work is the biggest thing anyone puts into a new studio, and the only contribution that leaves no receipt. Logging it is what turns hours into ownership.

What it is
Work on the business: wiring, acoustic treatment, buildout, maintenance, engineering sessions for outside clients, booking, bookkeeping, running the calendar, cleaning, hauling, social media, dealing with the landlord.
What it isn't
Work a partner does for themselves: their own records, their own band, their own clients.
Value
Hours logged × the rate for that kind of work, or the agreed value of a priced project.
How

By the hour. The partner logs date, task, and hours (half-hour rounding) the week they do it. Rate it by the kind of work, not by who did it: what the studio would pay a stranger for that job (example rates below). The manager approves at the close on whether it happened and whether it was studio work, not on how they'd have done it. Dollar value = hours × rate.

By the project. Work with a finish line (wiring, treatment, buildout, a website, a client's record) is priced like a contractor's quote, so the fast, skilled person is rewarded for being fast and skilled. Before starting, the person writes one line: what gets done, what "done" looks like, what it's worth. The manager agrees or counters (another partner, if the manager is doing it); over $1,000 it's a vote. The agreed line goes in the ledger with a target date. Scope changes get re-agreed before continuing, never after. On completion the manager checks it against the "done" line and the value goes in the ledger; no hours are logged for the same work. Half done is worth what the two agree, or nothing. Hourly stays for work with no end: booking, cleaning, maintenance. Pick the lane when the work starts.

Rules
  • No cap by default. If someone does the most work, they should own the most. A cap can be added by vote, but it's not the starting point.
  • Work unlogged after three months earns nothing. Backfill within three months is fine; past that, only a vote can reopen it. This is the rule that protects the heavy-work partner most, because it's what makes their number undeniable.
  • Partners still pay the monthly fee. Work earns ownership; it doesn't pay for the room. See Why do I have to pay a fee.
  • The manager's own hours are approved by another partner. Same reason the manager can't sign off their own contributions over the cap.
  • Work the studio didn't ask for can be held. Keep a shared to-do list; anything on it is pre-approved. Anything off it is at the manager's discretion, so check first before spending a weekend on it.
  • Pre-freeze, work is paid in slices only. No partner invoices the studio for their own work until the freeze. Reimbursing expenses is the one exception.
  • Turned-down paid work can be logged at the rate that was turned down. If a partner declines real paid work to do studio work and can show it (a written offer with a date and a rate), those hours are valued at the declined rate instead of the studio's band for that task, capped at the top of the engineering band and approved like any other line. The exchange rate doesn't change; the dollar value does. No evidence, normal rate.

Gear

Equipment a partner already owns.

What it is
Gear a partner sells or lends to the LLC for ownership. Sold means the title transfers and the partner gets slices, not money. Lent means the partner keeps the title and the studio's use of it earns a little every month.
What it isn't
Gear lent for free: still the partner's, earns nothing, goes home whenever they want. New gear bought with a partner's money: that's cash, and the LLC owns what it buys. Gear the studio buys from a partner for cash: that's a purchase, the partner got paid, nothing goes in the ledger. Gear the manager hasn't accepted. Personal gear a partner uses for their own sessions.
Value
Sold to the studio: the agreed resale value today, what it would actually sell for this week, logged once.
Lent to the studio: 3% of that resale value every month it's in the room and working.
How

Bringing it in. List the item and propose a resale value with a couple of comps, Reverb or eBay sold listings. The manager agrees or counters. Then pick one option for it: sold, lent for ownership, or lent free. The choice goes on the ledger line, and the item goes on the gear list as the LLC's if sold, or as a loan if lent.

Logging it. Sold gear is logged once, the day it comes in, at the agreed value. Lent-for-ownership gear earns its monthly amount at each monthly close for as long as it's in the room and working; the manager adds the line, not the partner.

Rules
  • The gear list is the record of ownership. If the list says the LLC owns it, it does; if it says a partner does, it walks out with them. The operating agreement treats it the way it treats the ledger.
  • The manager can refuse any item, on space, condition, need, or insurability. Nothing comes in without a yes. A partner who disagrees can take it to a regular vote, and the vote overrides the manager either way.
  • Value is agreed, not declared. If it's the manager's gear, another partner agrees the number.
  • Over $2,000 for ownership needs a vote.
  • One choice per item, no switching, no double dipping. Sold gear doesn't also earn rent.
  • Sold gear is the LLC's: it insures it, maintains it, and keeps it if the partner leaves, with a buyback right at the recorded value.
  • Lent gear stays the partner's: it walks out with them, and it stops accruing the month it leaves the room or breaks. The LLC insures it while it's there.
  • Under about $500, or personal gear at any value, is lent free by default. Rental slices on a mic stand are noise.

Business

Clients, deals, sponsorships, and funding a partner brings to the studio. A studio with no clients is a rehearsal space with a lease.

What it is
Outside money a partner brings in: a client who books the room, a venue deal, a sponsorship, a grant, a loan, an investor. Valued as the commission the studio would otherwise have paid a stranger to bring it.
What it isn't
A partner's own bookings; their use is covered by the fee. A client who booked and didn't pay. Money that hasn't landed yet. A commission taken as cash, which is money now instead of ownership. Engineering the client's session, which is work and is logged separately on top.
Value
Clients: 15% of what the client actually pays the studio, every month they keep paying. No cutoff.
Sponsorships, grants, and deals: 10% of the money that actually lands. It's the studio's to keep, like client revenue.
Loans and investment: 5% of the money that actually lands. Lower because it has to be paid back or paid for with ownership.
How

Claiming it. At the client's first booking, or when a deal is signed, the partner adds a line to the ledger: who the client or counterparty is, and that they brought them. Settled then, in writing, never argued about later.

Choosing ownership or cash. On that same line, the partner picks how the commission is taken: as a contribution (slices, nothing paid out, the partner owns more) or as cash (a finder's fee paid at the close, a studio expense, taxable income to the partner). One choice per client, not switched later.

Logging it. The manager adds the commission line at each monthly close from the actual payments received: client, amount paid, commission, ownership or cash. The partner doesn't log this one; the bank does.

Rules
  • Counted when the money lands, never when the introduction happens. A deposit counts when the deposit arrives.
  • Who brought a client is settled at the first booking. If two partners both claim one, they split it or the manager decides before the first session, not after.
  • Regulars and workers get cash only. The ownership option is for partners.
  • The rates change only by vote.
  • Pre-freeze, ownership is the default for a partner who doesn't choose. Post-freeze, it's all cash; slices are over.

Risk

Signing the lease guarantee. Someone's name has to be on the hook for the rent, and that's worth something.

What it is
A partner personally guaranteeing the lease: promising the landlord the rent if the LLC can't pay. Landlords require it from a brand-new company, and whoever signs is putting their own savings behind the studio for the whole term.
What it isn't
The ordinary risk every partner takes by contributing; that's already priced into the exchange rate. Co-signing a loan or putting up collateral for something else; those are separate and get their own agreed value if they ever happen. Being the manager, which is a job, not a risk.
Value
One to three months of rent, agreed up front by the partners before the lease is signed. At $2,000 rent, that's $2,000 to $6,000. Logged once.
How

Deciding who signs. Either the manager alone, or every partner in equal shares, since the pie is at zero on signing day. The partners vote on which before the lease, because the guarantee is a personal liability that outlasts the pie: it's there until the lease ends, whoever owns what by then.

Logging it. The day the lease is signed, one ledger line per guarantor at the agreed value, marked "risk." If several partners sign pro rata, each gets their proportion of the value.

Rules
  • The value is set before anyone signs, by vote, not negotiated afterward.
  • It's logged once, not monthly. The guarantee doesn't grow with time.
  • Leaving doesn't release it. A partner who leaves is still on the guarantee until the landlord releases them or the lease ends. The agreement should say the remaining partners will try to get them released, and that the studio indemnifies a departed guarantor if it ever gets called.
  • A new lease or renewal is a new guarantee and a new ledger line, agreed again.
  • If the guarantee is ever called, what the guarantor actually pays the landlord is a cash contribution, or a loan to the LLC, their choice.

Dollar values

Every contribution gets a number in dollars before anything else happens.

What it is
The dollar amount a contribution is worth to the studio, set the moment it happens.
Cash is its face value.
Work is hours × the rate for that kind of work, or an agreed project price.
Gear is today's resale value, once if sold, 3% a month if lent.
Business is a commission on money that landed.
Risk is one to three months of rent.
What it isn't
Not what something cost the partner. A $4,000 interface that resells for $2,500 is $2,500. Not what the partner charges elsewhere; work is valued at the job, not the person. Not a negotiation after the fact; the number is set when the contribution is logged.
Why
Five different kinds of thing can only be compared if they're all in the same unit first. Dollars are the unit everyone already understands, and most contributions have an obvious dollar figure attached: a receipt, a rate, a resale listing, a payment.
Rules
Values are agreed, not declared: with the manager, or with another partner when it's the manager's own. Once logged and approved, a value doesn't get revisited. The rates and percentages that produce these numbers are listed in Money math and change only by vote.

Monthly close

Once a month, every partner's contributions get converted to slices.

Approve
The manager goes through every contribution logged since the last close and marks it approved, held, or disputed, checking against the bank, the receipt, the calendar, or the work. Only approved lines go further.
Convert
Each approved contribution's type is what determines its dollar value and its exchange rate. The formula for slices:

slices = dollar value × exchange rate

Rules
  • Only approved contributions convert. A held one converts at the close it's finally approved, dated back to when it happened.
  • A contribution converts once. No re-running old months when a rate changes later.
  • One exchange rate for everyone, set by vote before anyone contributes and written into the operating agreement. Changing it applies going forward only.
  • A partner who actually turns down paid work for the studio logs those hours at the rate they turned down, with evidence. The exchange rate doesn't change; the dollar value does. See the Work contribution rules.
  • The manager's own contributions are approved by another partner.
  • The close happens on a fixed date, set at the partner meeting, whether or not there's much to close. Converting contributions is one step of the manager's routine that month; the full checklist is in the manual's monthly close.
  • If the posted split looks wrong, say so that month, not next year. The ledger and the current split are visible to every partner at any time (see What partners do at the close).
What this adds up to
This is the whole ownership model, run once a month. A partner puts something into the studio. It gets a dollar value the day it happens and a line in the ledger. At the close, that dollar value becomes slices, and the slices are added to everything that partner has put in before. Their share of the studio is that total, as a percentage of everyone's. Every month the pie is recalculated from what people actually did, so the split is never a guess and never stale. It keeps moving until the studio can pay for its own work; then it freezes, the percentages become units, and from that day the studio pays in money instead of ownership. Contributions in, ownership out, with the monthly close as the turn of the crank.

Partner tax

Bottom line: the money you put into the studio isn't taxed, and for the first stretch being a partner lowers your taxes rather than raising them. Each spring you get one extra form, your K-1, and its numbers go on the return you already file; the first one covers 2027 and arrives by March 2028. Nothing else about how you do your taxes changes.

Pre-freeze, you owe nothing on the studio and your K-1 shows a loss. If you actively work in the studio, that loss reduces your federal taxable income. Your monthly fee is a deductible business expense on top of that.

Post-freeze, you pay tax on your share of the profit, at your own rate, the same way you'd pay on any other income. The studio sends you cash each quarter to cover it.

Your other income is untouched. Gigs, sessions, a day job: the studio doesn't change how any of that is reported. The one wrinkle: a K-1 loss can offset it federally, not in New Jersey.

Cash gigs are your business, same as they were. The studio doesn't report them, see them, or need to know about them. The only thing that shows up anywhere is what you put into the ledger.

Owning slices isn't income. Nobody gets a tax bill for ownership they earned by working.

You can't raise your deduction by paying a bigger fee. A fee is deductible because it's a reasonable price for something you use; a partner paying $1,000 for the same access others get for $200 is making an $800 contribution with a different label, and the IRS reads substance, not labels. The extra would also become studio income shared across everyone by percentage, so you'd hand part of your deduction to the other partners and get no ownership for it. If you want to put in more, it's a contribution, and it buys slices. The fee only goes up when the partners vote it up for everyone.

For a partner, what's taxed is their percentage of the studio's net profit: their ownership share of what's left after rent, bills, and every other expense, reported on their own return whether or not any cash was paid out. A 30% partner is taxed on 30% of the net profit. Revenue isn't taxed. Contributions aren't. Distributions aren't; the profit they come from already was. In a losing year nothing is owed, and each partner's percentage of the loss passes through to them instead. The things that don't depend on profit:
  • Guaranteed payments (post-freeze). Pay to a partner for work is taxable to them even in a year the studio loses money. It's an expense to the studio.
  • Self-employment tax (post-freeze, in practice). Owed by active partners on their share of profit, so only if there is profit.
  • New Jersey's per-owner fee and the annual report fee. Flat, every year, profit or not. They're the studio's bills, paid from the studio's account, never passed to partners.
  • Sales tax on taxable lines like gear rental to a client. Collected from the client and passed to the state; never the studio's money.
  • Payroll taxes (post-freeze), once there's an employee. Not until then.

A loss is the same table run the other way: a year where the expense column outweighs the revenue column. Nothing in the neither column changes it. Pre-freeze that's the normal picture: fees in, rent and bills out, a loss that each partner carries onto their own return. No income tax, just the flat state fee the studio pays.

Pre-freeze taxes

The studio loses money on paper, and almost nothing is owed.

The studio
Fees come in, rent and bills go out, so the year ends in a loss. No income tax. It still pays New Jersey's flat fees, the $150 per owner and the annual report, and collects sales tax on any taxable client lines. The loss doesn't sit with the studio; it passes through to the partners.
Each partner
  • The K-1 shows a share of the loss. Active partners deduct it federally against their other income, capped at their basis. New Jersey allows none of it.
  • Nothing is taxed on slices. Earning ownership for work, gear, business, or risk is not income while the pie is running.
  • Contributions aren't deductible; the monthly fee is. That's the only money sent to the studio that comes back on a return.
  • No self-employment tax, no quarterly estimates. There's no profit share to owe them on.
Rules
The first K-1 covers 2027. If the studio takes the early-start path, the gap months go on the manager's own Schedule C and 2026 still has no K-1s.

Post-freeze taxes

The studio makes money, and the partners pay tax on it.

The studio
Still no income tax of its own. It pays partners for work as guaranteed payments and contractors on 1099s, both deductible expenses; runs payroll once there's an employee; keeps collecting sales tax. Each quarter it sends tax distributions, enough for every partner to cover the tax on their share, before adding to the reserve.
Each partner
  • The K-1 shows a share of the profit, by units. Income tax on it at the partner's own rate, plus self-employment tax if active, whether or not the cash was distributed.
  • Pay for work is taxed to whoever earned it, as a guaranteed payment, before the rest of the profit is split.
  • Cash finder's fees, loan interest, and cash gear rental are ordinary income to the partner receiving them.
  • Distributions aren't taxed again. The profit they come from already was.
  • Quarterly estimated payments, federal and state, once a share creates a tax bill.
Rules
The freeze itself shouldn't be a taxable event: slices become units at each partner's exact percentage, and nobody receives anything new. The lawyer and accountant confirm how the conversion is papered so it stays that way.
Electing S-corp

Post-freeze, once the studio is profitable and at least one working partner's share of profit is clearing around $40,000 a year, the partners vote to elect S-corp tax treatment on the accountant's recommendation. What that means: the LLC stays exactly what it is, but working partners start drawing a W-2 salary, the rest of the profit is distributed by units, and the self-employment tax that would have applied to the whole profit share applies only to the salary.

Why. Self-employment tax. As a partnership, active partners owe roughly 15% on their entire profit share. As an S-corp, that tax applies only to the salary; the distributions on top are free of it. Once a partner's share is large enough, that saving beats the cost of running payroll.

When. Not before the freeze. An S-corp has to split profit strictly by ownership, every day of the year, so it can't handle percentages that move monthly. After the freeze, the rule of thumb is when a partner's profit share is clearing around $40,000 a year; below that, the payroll costs eat the savings. The election is due by March 15 to apply to that year.

Costs and catches.

  • Payroll. A payroll service, W-2s, quarterly filings, workers' comp and unemployment insurance. Real money and real admin.
  • Reasonable compensation. The IRS requires that working partners be paid a market salary before taking distributions. Paying yourself $5,000 and distributing $80,000 is the audit flag.
  • One class of ownership. Every unit has to carry the same economic rights. Non-voting units are fine; units with different profit rights aren't. The reserved pool and buyout terms need to be drafted with this in mind.
  • Guaranteed payments go away; partners who work become employees of the company for tax purposes, which is the one exception to the rule that partners can't be employees.

Rules. New Jersey now follows the federal election automatically, so there's no separate state filing, but the accountant confirms. The decision is a regular vote, on the accountant's recommendation, and it can be undone later if it stops making sense.

Ownership tax split

By ownership share, the same way profit is.

By the pie. Profit and loss follow ownership. A 40% partner reports 40% of the year's result on their own return and pays at their own rate, so two partners with the same share can owe different amounts.

One K-1 each. The LLC files an informational return in March and sends every partner a K-1 with their share. The LLC itself pays nothing (see Documents).

Pre-freeze, the share moves. Each month's result is allocated by that month's percentages from the monthly close. That's what the law requires when ownership changes during the year, and the close already produces the numbers.

Post-freeze, pay for work comes first. Guaranteed payments to a partner are taxed to that partner alone; what's left is split by percentage. Active partners owe self-employment tax on their share, passive partners don't, and once there's real profit electing S-corp changes that math.

Are contributions deductible?

No. The monthly fee is.

Why
A contribution is an investment in something you own, and there's no deduction for that. It raises your basis, your tax record of what you've put in, and comes back to you only as your share of the studio's losses. The fee is a payment for a service, which is why it's deductible the day you pay it, federally and in New Jersey.

Losses flow through, if you're active

What
While the studio loses money, each partner's K-1 shows their share of the loss. Partners who actively work in the studio deduct it against their other income on their federal return. Passive partners carry it forward against future studio profit instead.
Rules
  • Active means real work for the business: sessions for clients, booking, maintenance, buildout. Recording your own music doesn't count. The work log is the evidence.
  • Capped at basis. You can't deduct more loss than you've put in; the rest carries forward.
  • New Jersey allows none of it. The state won't net a partnership loss against wages or music income.
Example
For a working musician with self-employment income, a $10,000 loss share is worth roughly $3,000 to $4,000 federally, because it reduces both income tax and self-employment tax. In New Jersey, $0.

Ownership for work isn't taxed until the freeze

What
Receiving ownership for work can be taxable income. The plan avoids that by issuing nothing until the freeze: pre-freeze, slices are a contractual formula in the operating agreement, not units anyone holds.
Rules
If the lawyer instead sets up profits interests at formation, each recipient files an 83(b) election within 30 days of the grant, no extensions, and the manager tells them the date.

New Jersey: a loss in one category can't offset income in another

What
New Jersey taxes wages, self-employment income, and partnership income as separate categories. A partnership loss cannot reduce your wages or your Schedule C music income on the state return. It's simply gone for state purposes.
Why
It's how the state's income tax is written. Most states follow the federal "one pot" approach; New Jersey doesn't.
How
This is the whole reason for the hybrid money model. A fee for studio time is a business expense inside your music income category, so it is deductible in New Jersey. A contribution that comes back as a partnership loss isn't.
Specifics
The federal deduction still works either way. Only the state treatment differs.

New Jersey: $150 per owner per year

What
New Jersey charges a partnership with more than two owners $150 per owner every year, including loss years, plus a half-year prepayment the first year. The studio pays it, not you. It comes out of the LLC account like rent or insurance. No partner writes a $150 check or sees it on their own tax bill.
Why
It's a state filing fee on the partnership itself, sized by headcount. Four owners: the studio pays $900 the first year, $600 a year after. Two owners: nothing.
How
Everyone who holds slices is a legal member of the LLC, and the studio pays the fee as an ordinary expense. There's a tempting workaround (keep two legal members, hold everyone else's share as a contractual promise) and it's rejected here: a partner who isn't a legal member owns nothing in law, gets no K-1 and no loss deduction, and would face a tax event when units are finally issued. Saving $150 a person isn't worth that.
Specifics
Paid by the LLC with its state return, due with the annual partnership filing. It's a line in the studio's budget, same as the annual report fee. Nothing is passed through to partners. Budget $600 to $900 a year at four partners.

New Jersey: sales tax on studio time

What
New Jersey sales tax is 6.625%. Renting a room (real property) is generally not taxable. Renting equipment is. A bundled rate that delivers a tangible product may be taxable as a whole.
Why
The state taxes tangible goods and some services. Space isn't a tangible good; a rented microphone is.
How
Invoices to clients list any gear leaving the building on its own line; gear used in the room is part of the room. Partner monthly fees are invoiced as studio access, which should fall under room use; the accountant confirms.
Specifics
No New Jersey ruling on recording studios specifically was found. The accountant confirms with the Division of Taxation before the first paid outside session.

7The fee

Every partner pays the studio the same flat amount each month for the room. It's a customer payment, not an investment: it covers rent, it's deductible, and it never buys ownership.

Why does everyone pay for time?

What
Nobody uses the studio for free because they own part of it. Partners, including the manager, pay the LLC a monthly fee for access. Outside clients pay by the hour or per session. Either way the money is a payment for use of the room, not an investment in the company.
Why
Two reasons. First, it's what covers rent predictably: fees come in, rent goes out. Second, taxes. For a working musician, a fee for studio time is an ordinary business expense, deductible against music income both federally and in New Jersey. If the same money went in as an ownership contribution instead, New Jersey wouldn't let you deduct it (see Taxes).
How
The LLC invoices each person monthly. Payment goes into the LLC's bank account, tagged as a fee. Each person keeps the invoice for their own taxes.
Specifics
The invoice says "studio access" and the month, not "your share of the rent." It's not the word rent that matters; renting a room by the hour from a stranger is deductible too. It's customer versus owner. A payment for using the room is an expense to you and income to the studio, deductible. An owner chipping in for the company's rent is a capital contribution, an investment, and New Jersey won't let that come back as a deduction. The invoice should describe which one it is.
Tax notes
The fee is income to the studio and a deductible business expense for the partner paying it, federally and in New Jersey, as long as the invoice says what it is: studio access, not a share of the rent. A client's hourly payment is income to the studio and the client's own business expense.

How is the monthly fee set?

What
Partners pay one fixed amount per month and can use the room as much as the calendar allows. Outside clients pay hourly or per session. A regular who isn't a partner could be offered a higher flat monthly fee.
Why
It's the partner perk, and it's what makes "no hour tracking" possible. A flat rate means nobody counts anyone's hours.
How

The fee lives in a band. The floor is what every partner can reliably pay each month. The ceiling is what the access is actually worth: what a regular who owns nothing, or a lockout room nearby, would pay for unlimited use of a space like this. Anywhere in that band is a legitimate, fully deductible fee. Above the ceiling, the extra is a contribution with a different label.

Where in the band is a trade. Every dollar of fee is a deduction and zero ownership. Every dollar of contribution is ownership and zero deduction. A crew that wants deductions sets the fee near the ceiling; a crew that wants to earn the studio sets it near the floor and contributes the rest. Whatever's left to cover rent comes from contributions (see What if not everyone can cover the same share of rent?).

Written into the operating agreement as a benefit of being a partner, and changed only by vote.

Rules
Same fee for every partner, regardless of ownership or income. Don't do "you pay $200, you pay $600": the $600 partner would be paying $400 extra as a fee and getting nothing for it. One flat fee inside the band; anything above it is a contribution that buys ownership. Revisit the fee by vote when client income grows or partners' situations change.
Tax notes
The fee is deductible for the partner; anything paid above it is a contribution and isn't. Setting the fee nearer the ceiling keeps more of what everyone pays deductible; setting it nearer the floor turns more into ownership. Letting the rest be contributions means the money that stays deductible for the people who can least afford to lose the deduction.

Why doesn't the fee earn ownership?

What
Your monthly fee buys use of the room. It does not buy a piece of the company. Ownership comes only from what you put in beyond the fee.
Why
Keeps the two things clean. It also answers the question "what happens to the rent I paid if I leave?" Nothing. It paid for the months you used the room, same as anyone renting anything.
How
Every deposit into the LLC account gets one of two tags in the books: fee or contribution. A contribution also gets its own line in the ledger. The monthly fee is always tagged as a fee. Anything above it is a contribution and earns ownership.
Rules
Anything you pay above your fee is a contribution by default, which earns ownership. The only reason to pay extra as a fee is for something that genuinely costs the studio, like consumables you used, and you'd say so. Extra fee money otherwise just becomes profit shared with everyone.
Tax notes
This is the tax line too. Fee: deductible for you, income to the studio. Contribution: not deductible, raises your basis, comes back only as a share of the studio's loss, which New Jersey won't let you use. The tag on the deposit decides which.

Why do I have to pay a fee if I'm doing all the work?

What
Because slices don't pay the landlord. Rent is cash, due every month. If you put in zero cash, another partner covers your share of the room, and they own more for doing it. "Not paying" doesn't save you anything. It moves ownership from you to whoever covers you, at 4x.
Why
  • Everyone who uses the room pays for it, including the manager. It's the one rule with no exceptions, which is exactly why it can be trusted.
  • The fee is set where everyone can reliably pay it. If $400 is above the floor, the fee is lower. If $200 is too much, say the number. It's the same for everyone and nobody is priced out.
  • Your work is on top of the fee, not instead of it. Pay the fee, log your hours, and in the six-month example the builder is the biggest owner in the room without putting in a dollar beyond the fee.
How
  • Working off the fee would be worse for you. An hour of work logged as a contribution earns slices at 2x, forever. The same hour used to pay down a fee is worth $50 once and then it's gone. It would also be barter income, taxable to you.
  • The fee comes back on your taxes. It's a business expense against your music income, federally and in New Jersey. A contribution isn't deductible. The fee is the version of the money that comes back.
Rules
If you genuinely can't pay one month, nothing happens (see What if I can't pay one month?). If the partners want to waive someone's fee long-term, that's a vote. The gap is covered by the others, who earn slices for it. That's the trade, and it's yours to make with eyes open.
Tax notes
Paying the fee and logging the hours is the tax-best version for you: the fee is deductible, and the hours earn ownership that isn't taxed until the freeze. Working off the fee instead would be barter income, taxable to you, with no deduction.

What if not everyone can cover the same share of rent?

What
Some months, or some partners, will cover more of the rent than others. There's no separate rule for this. The ownership calculation absorbs it.
Why
Musician income is uneven, and not everyone can pay the same amount. A model that needs everyone to pay exactly the same every month would break in month two. Instead, the fee is one number in a band everyone can reliably pay, and the pie handles everything above or below it. The partners who can put in more own more. The partners who can't still have full access and aren't in default; if they're putting in work, their hours are earning ownership the whole time.
How
  • Pays the fee: that's a fee. Covers their share of running the room. No slices, full deduction.
  • Pays more than the fee: the extra is tagged as a contribution, earns slices at 4x, and goes toward rent or the reserve. They own more for it.
  • Pays less than the fee, or nothing: no slices that month, no penalty. The reserve covers the gap. At 90 days unpaid it becomes a capital call; the partners who cover it get the slices, and the unpaid partner's share dilutes.
  • The fees don't add up to the rent (early on, few partners): the shortfall is a capital call split by current ownership. Whoever actually pays earns slices. Someone who carries it every month ends up owning more every month.
Specifics
  • The part above the fee is a contribution, not a fee, so it isn't deductible in New Jersey. The person gets ownership instead of a deduction. If they'd rather have the deduction, the alternative is a vote to raise their fee, which makes it all fee and earns no extra ownership.
  • Memo line on the transfer says which is which: "studio access, November" for the fee, "contribution" for anything above it.
  • If one partner is carrying the rent month after month, that's a sign the fee is too low or there aren't enough partners, and it should come up at the close.
Tax notes
Whoever covers more than the fee is making a contribution, not paying a fee, so the extra isn't deductible in New Jersey; they get ownership instead. Covering a gap as a loan to the LLC is neither: no deduction, no slices, repaid later.

What if I can't pay one month?

What
Nothing bad happens. If a partner can't pay their monthly fee one month, they just don't earn any extra ownership that month, and the studio covers it from the reserve.
Why
Musician income is lumpy. A structure that kicks people out for one slow month isn't a partnership.
How
Tell the manager before the due date, or at the close before it. The shortfall is noted. Nothing accrues against you.
Rules
At 90 days unpaid, the capital-call clause kicks in: the other partners cover the gap and the unpaid partner's share is diluted by the ordinary pie math. That's the only penalty, and it's three months out.

How is the fee taxed?

A customer payment for the room. Deductible for you, income for the studio.

It's a business expense. You pay the LLC a flat monthly fee for studio access, the same way a client pays by the hour. For a working musician that's an ordinary expense against music income, deductible federally and in New Jersey the day you pay it. Keep the invoice; that's the record.

It's income to the studio. Every fee lands on the LLC's books as revenue. It makes the studio's loss smaller (or its profit bigger), and that result flows back to all partners by ownership share.

It's not a contribution. Fee money never buys ownership, and it never raises your basis. The deposit is tagged "fee" in the books; anything above the fee is tagged "contribution," gets a line in the ledger, and is not deductible. The tag is what the accountant reads.

The invoice says what it is. "Studio access" and the month, not "your share of the rent." Customer versus owner is the whole distinction: a payment for using the room is an expense; an owner chipping in for the company's rent is an investment, and New Jersey won't let that come back as a deduction.

You can't deduct more by paying more. A fee is deductible because it's a reasonable price for something you use. A partner paying $1,000 for the same access others get for $200 is making an $800 contribution with a different label, and the IRS reads substance. The extra would also become studio income shared with everyone by percentage, so you'd hand part of it to the other partners and get no ownership for it.

No sales tax on it. Renting space isn't taxable in New Jersey; renting gear is. The fee is invoiced as studio access, which should fall under room use. The accountant confirms before the first invoice (see Sales tax).

Who can deduct it. Anyone with music income reported as a business (Schedule C). If your only income is a W-2 job and music is a hobby, the fee isn't deductible for you; it's still the price of the room, and it still isn't ownership.

Why is the exchange rate 4 and 2?

What it is
The multiplier applied to every dollar value: cash counts at 4x; work, gear, business, and risk count at 2x. Only the ratio matters: cash earns twice the slices per dollar that anything else does, so one hour of $50 work and $25 of cash earn the same ownership. It's not a markdown of work; the hourly rate does its full job first. And it's not a law; it's the group's choice, written down before anyone contributes.
Why 4 and 2
Three reasons cash deserves more per dollar than work at the gross rate. Each one is a factor, and the factors multiply.

What happens when the studio runs short?

What
If the studio genuinely runs short (reserve is gone, rent is due), the manager can call for partners to put in money.
Why
Rent doesn't wait. The alternative is the manager personally covering it, which is how resentment starts.
How

The call. Split by current ownership share. Money paid in is a cash contribution at 4x, like any other. A partner who can't pay isn't penalized for 90 days; the reserve covers the gap.

How a non-payer gets diluted, pre-freeze. No formula needed; it's the ordinary pie math. The partners who pay earn slices for their cash, and whoever covers the non-payer's share earns slices for that too. The non-payer's slice count doesn't move while everyone else's grows, so their percentage falls. Example: a $1,200 call split 600 / 400 / 200. A and B pay theirs and A covers C's 200. A earns 3,200 slices, B earns 1,600, C earns none, and C now owns a smaller piece of a bigger pie.

Post-freeze, with units fixed, the operating agreement supplies the formula: the partners who cover the shortfall are either issued units from the reserved pool at the agreed price, or treated as having lent the LLC the money, repaid before any distributions.

Specifics
Capital calls should be rare. If they're happening every quarter, the monthly fee is set too low and should be voted up.
Tax notes
Money paid into a capital call is a contribution: not deductible, raises your basis. After the freeze, covering a shortfall is a loan to the LLC; the repayment isn't income, and any interest the partners agree on is.

Where does the money go?

What
Fees and client income come in (see Moving money). Rent, insurance, utilities, repairs, and the state's annual fees go out. What's left builds a reserve. Only money beyond the reserve target is profit.
Why
A studio with no cushion folds the first month a partner is short or a piece of gear dies. The reserve is what makes "no penalty for a bad month" affordable.
How
Reserve target is two to three months of rent. Until it's there, nothing is distributed. Once it's there, surplus is distributed by ownership share. (This is cash. The reserved pool in the ownership section is units, a different thing.)
Specifics
While the studio is losing money (which is normal for the first stretch), nobody receives anything. Extra income just makes the loss smaller. Partner contributions are never "profit," no matter how much comes in; they're capital, and the contributor got ownership for them. New Jersey's per-owner fee and the annual report are studio bills like rent: paid from the account, never passed to partners.
Tax notes
Profit is taxed to partners when the studio earns it, not when it's paid out. Money kept in the reserve is still taxed. That's why, once profitable, the studio distributes enough each quarter to cover everyone's tax on their share before building the reserve further.

Voting: who decides what

The manager decides day-to-day things alone. A short list of big things needs a vote.
Why
Protects partners from a manager going rogue, and protects the manager from needing a meeting to buy cables.

"Majority of units" means more than half the ownership. "At least two people" means a single big owner can't act alone even if they hold a majority. Both conditions together protect small and large partners.

Step 1: the ledger settles facts

What
Most disputes are about what someone did or paid. The ledger is the record. If it's in the ledger, it happened. If it isn't, it didn't.
Why
Arguments about memory are unwinnable. Arguments about a shared log are short.
How
Raise it at the monthly close, or sooner if it can't wait. The manager pulls up the entry. If an entry is wrong, it's corrected that month, with a note.
Rules
Disputes about an entry older than three months are presumed settled. That's why everyone checks the posted split every month.

Step 2: talk, then vote

What
Disagreements about what to do (buy this, change the fee, let this person in) go to a conversation first, then to a vote under the rules in Voting.
Why
Most things aren't votes. They're a ten-minute conversation. The vote is the backstop, not the first move.
How
Day-to-day: the manager decides, and a partner who disagrees says so at the close. Listed big decisions: majority of units and at least two people. Lease, sale, dissolution: everyone.
Specifics
The "at least two people" rule is the anti-steamroll clause. Even a partner holding 60% can't pass a big decision alone. And a partner holding 5% can't block one alone either. It takes two to pass and two to block.

Step 3: the manager can be replaced

What
If the dispute is with the manager, partners can remove and replace the manager by vote.
Why
Manager-managed only works if the manager answers to someone. Without this clause, the role is a throne.
How
Same threshold as other big decisions: majority of units and at least two people, not counting the manager's own units if the vote is about removing them.
Rules
Removal as manager doesn't touch their ownership. They keep their units and their slices. They just stop running day to day.

Step 4: deadlock

What
If a vote can't pass or block, or two partners are at a true standstill, the agreement provides a way out that doesn't involve a courtroom.
Why
Court is slow, public, and costs more than the studio is worth. The agreement should make it the last resort by giving everyone a cheaper path first.
How
  • Mediation first. A neutral third party, split cost, one session. Non-binding, but it ends most things.
  • Arbitration second. A private, binding decision by an arbitrator instead of a judge. Faster and cheaper than court. The agreement names the rules (e.g. AAA commercial) so there's no fight about the process.
  • Usage disputes go through the tripwire in Booking the studio, not this ladder.
Specifics
A clause that says "all disputes under this agreement go to mediation, then binding arbitration in New Jersey" is standard and the lawyer will have one. Partners give up the right to sue each other in court over the agreement, which is the point.

The nuclear option: a shotgun clause

What
For a deadlock between two partners that can't be resolved any other way: one partner names a price per unit. The other must either buy the first partner out at that price, or sell their own units at that price. The one who names the number doesn't get to pick which.
Why
It forces an honest price. Name it too high and you'll be forced to buy at it. Name it too low and you'll be forced to sell at it.
How
Written notice, a set window (say 30 days) to choose, payment terms per the buyout rules in Leaving the company.
Specifics
Brutal, and rarely used. Its real job is to exist, so nobody drags a deadlock out for a year. Optional; decide with the lawyer whether to include it.

10Leaving the company

You're not trapped. A partner can leave at any time, with notice, and keeps what they earned. This is what that looks like before and after the freeze.

How to leave

Say so in writing, to the manager, with a date. Thirty days is the default so the calendar, the fee, and the gear can be sorted; sooner by agreement.

Your last fee is for your last month. Nothing is owed after that, and nothing is owed on the way out.

Your gear goes with you. Anything on the loan list leaves the day you do. Gear you sold to the studio can be bought back at its recorded value if you want it.

You stop being a legal member on your date; the lawyer's paperwork is a one-page withdrawal. What you earned stays as an economic interest, which New Jersey law recognizes for a partner who has left: a right to the money, no vote. The card below says what that looks like for the phase the studio is in.

Before the freeze: you keep what you earned

What
Your slices stay on the ledger as a non-voting stake. If the studio later becomes profitable, you get your share of distributions, or the partners buy you out then at the formula below. Leaving doesn't erase what you put in.
Why
Pre-freeze, the studio has no cash to hand anyone; what everyone put in is in the room. A claim on future profit is the honest version of "you'll get yours": it pays out exactly when the studio can, instead of promising a number it can't produce.
How
Every slice stays: cash, gear, business, risk, and work. The hours were done and the money was spent whether you stay or go. A priced project you walk away from half-finished is logged at the value of what was actually done, like any other. The only deduction anywhere is for removal for cause, and even then it's the damage, not the slices.
Rules
The remaining partners can offer a fixed amount, paid over time, to cancel the claim entirely, but only if the leaving partner agrees. Nobody can be forced to sell a pre-freeze claim.

After the freeze: a buyout

What
Once the studio is profitable and ownership is in units, a leaving partner's units are bought back at a price set by formula, in cash, over time.
Why
A formula agreed in advance beats a negotiation during a breakup, and paying over time means a departure never starves the rent.
How
Proposed: 1.5x the studio's annual profit, times the partner's ownership share. If either side disputes it, each picks an appraiser and the two numbers are averaged.
Rules
Paid over 24 months, never as a lump sum, and capped at one third of the studio's distributable cash in any quarter. The units are held by the LLC, not sold to an outsider, unless the partners agree.

Removal

The one exit that isn't the partner's choice. Even then, earned is earned.

The partners can vote a partner out. Same threshold as any big decision, a majority of units and at least two people, not counting the partner in question (see Voting). It's rare, and it's a last resort after the dispute ladder.

Removed, you leave like anyone else. Everything you earned stays yours: the claim before the freeze, the buyout after it. The wiring is in the wall whether you're still a partner or not, and so is every other contribution. Nobody forfeits slices, for any reason.

Removed for cause, the damage comes off your claim. What you took, what you cost the studio, or the capital call you didn't pay is deducted from your claim or buyout, dollar for dollar, and whatever is left is still yours. That's the consequence: paying for the harm, not losing the work.

"Cause" is a short, specific list, because it's the only thing that lets the studio deduct anything, and if it's vague it becomes a weapon. Proposed: theft or fraud; a capital call unpaid for 90+ days after notice; conduct that gets the studio evicted or uninsurable.

Not cause: being hard to work with, using the room a lot, disagreeing with the manager. Those are reasons to vote on something, never reasons to touch anyone's ownership.
